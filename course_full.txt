ADULT ACNE
Although most common during the teenage years,
acne may also appear for the first time in midlife or worsen then. Acne results from inflammation around the hair follicles and oil-producing sebaceous glands of the skin.
Hormones known as androgens (the so-called male hormones), which increase during puberty in both males and females, contribute to acne. Hormonal fluctuations associated with menstruation and menopause make women more susceptible. Frequently touching the face—which we do approximately 16 times an hour without even realizing it—can also exacerbate acne (not to mention increase your risk for colds, flu, and COVID-19).
Click on the "Expand" button in each box to learn more.
SIGNS AND SYMPTOMS
These are the common signs
experienced by people with adult acne:
• Whiteheads
• Blackheads
• Papules and pustules (swollen red bumps), sometimes filled with pus.
Obencem / iStock / Getty Images
FIRST-LINE TREATMENTS FOR ADULT ACNE
Expand
BASIC ACNE TREATMENT
A number of treatments are available
to clear up acne,
including some that you apply to your skin and others you take in pill form.
First-line topical treatments typically fall into one of the following two categories (each described below):
• Salicylic acid washes
• Benzoyl peroxide gels, lotions, and washes.
Expand
SALICYCLIC ACID WASHES
These formulas loosen dead skin cells
and help dislodge plugs from pore openings, seen as whiteheads and blackheads. Salicylic acid washes are available in over-the-counter and prescription formulations, but the prescription versions aren’t any stronger.
Expand
BENZOYL PEROXIDE GELS, LOTIONS, AND WASHES
These products fight bacterial growth
and may help clear blocked pores. They are available in over-the-counter and prescription versions. They can induce side effects such as dry and peeling skin if the formulation is too strong or is used excessively, or if a person has very sensitive skin.
PRESCRIPTIONS FOR MORE SERIOUS OR ACTIVE CASES
Expand
TOPICAL RETINOIDS
Available as creams, gels, or liquids,
these drugs are very effective at clearing the skin of plugged follicles by increasing the turnover of skin cells.
The most common one, tretinoin (Retin-A), may cause skin irritation. To counter this effect, a new formulation of tretinoin called Altreno is compounded with a moisturizing lotion.
Other topical retinoids include adapalene (Differin), the first topical retinoid available over the counter (in a 0.1% formulation, although there is also a 0.3% gel available by prescription), tazarotene (Avage, Tazorac), and trifarotene (Aklief).
To avoid side effects such as drying, peeling, and redness, begin by using retinoids twice a week at night and gradually move up to every-other-night or nightly use. Because these medications may increase the skin’s sensitivity to sunlight, while you are using them always be sure to apply sunscreen during the day.
Expand
ORAL RETINOIDS
Isotretinoin (Amnesteem, Claravis, others)
is used to treat chronic, severe or scarring acne. Because this medication has been associated with severe birth defects, people taking it must be enrolled in a national risk evaluation and mitigation strategy program, where they are closely monitored by a dermatologist.
Expand
ANTIBIOTICS
Certain antibiotics
(available as creams, gels, lotions, or combination products) combined with other compounds can be applied directly to the skin to reduce the growth of acne-causing bacteria.
Examples include:
• erythromycin plus benzoyl peroxide (Benzamycin)
• clindamycin plus benzoyl peroxide (Duac, others)
• dapsone (Aczone).
Some cases are also treated with oral antibiotics, such as doxycycline (Oracea, Vibramycin, others) and minocycline (Minocin, others).
Expand
HORMONAL THERAPIES
Some birth control pills
lower androgen levels and help clear up acne in women. These include formulations that contain ethinyl estradiol and norgestimate (Ortho-Cyclen, Ortho Tri-Cyclen, generic) and ethinyl estradiol and drospirenone (Beyaz, Safyral, Yasmin, Yaz, generic).
Spironolactone (Aldactone, generic) is another hormonal medication that can be used in women. The drug is FDA-approved to treat high blood pressure, but it has also been found to block the effects of androgens on the sebaceous glands and is used “off-label” to manage acne.
Clascoterone (Winlevi), a topical cream that also inhibits androgens and may be used by both women and men, became FDA-approved in August 2020. One hope is that clascoterone may help to reduce the need for oral drugs to treat acne.
Expand
LASER AND RED AND BLUE LIGHT THERAPIES
There is little research
to support the use of laser and light therapies, especially for weaker devices that can be used at home. They’ve shown mixed results in clinical trials, and more study is needed to establish any benefits.

DOCTOR OR AESTHETICIAN?
Some procedures that affect only superficial layers of skin
can be performed by nurses or skin aestheticians, while others require the skills of a dermatologist or other physician. Whom you trust to perform a procedure is a crucial factor in whether the outcome will be successful. Click on the "Expand" button in each box below to learn more.
WHO DOES WHAT
L
ess invasive procedures
like microdermabrasion can be performed by cosmetology professionals. Skin surgeries, including laser procedures, should be performed by physicians, including:
• Dermatologists or dermatologic surgeons
• Plastic surgeons
• Facial plastic surgeons (otolaryngologists)
• Oculoplastic surgeons (ophthalmologists).
In some settings, nurses or physician assistants with special training may perform some of these procedures. Beware of laser and filler treatments at “medspas” that are performed by technicians with minimal training.
Above: Jose Luis Pelaez Inc / DigitalVision / Getty Images
CHOOSING A DOCTOR OR OTHER SKIN SPECIALIST
Expand
Start with a dermatologist—and check credentials
One way to start treatment
is with a dermatologist who can advise you about which procedures are best handled by a physician. Some physicians who perform cosmetic procedures also have nurses or aestheticians or both on staff to handle the less invasive treatments.
To find a reputable physician, begin by asking your own doctor for recommendations. Or contact professional societies that have sophisticated referral services and websites with information about physicians’ training and experience, the articles they’ve written, and the procedures they perform. In some areas, city or county medical associations can provide names of local physicians.
You can also ask friends, co-workers, or family members who’ve had cosmetic procedures if they were satisfied with the results and whether they would go back to the same physician. But remember that doctors who are popular or who advertise heavily may not be highly skilled, or they may not be right for you.
Just below, you'll find information on the American Board of Medical Specialties (ABMS), a worthy source for finding a physician.
Zolak / Stock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
American Board of Medical Specialties (ABMS)
Once you have the name of a physician,
the next step is to check whether he or she is board-certified. Many consumers know to ask about certification, but might not ask which board has done the certifying, or whether that board is overseen by the
American Board of Medical Specialties (ABMS)
.
The ABMS oversees 24 approved medical specialty boards in the United States. A number of other groups may call themselves “boards,” but they aren’t overseen by the ABMS. Their certification requirements may be less rigorous than boards that are under ABMS purview. More than 100 of these self-designated boards exist throughout the United States, and there are virtually no legal requirements for their formation. Some may be no more than glorified public relations offices. To check qualifications, contact the ABMS’s Doctor Verification Service at 312-436-2600, or online at
abms.org
.
Expand
American Board of Dermatology
A founding member of the ABMS
,
the American Board of Dermatology
certifies physicians to perform both general and specialty dermatologic care.
Physicians certified by this board have a medical degree from an accredited institution, at least one year of training in general medicine, and three years of additional training in dermatology. Some dermatologists pursue an additional year or two of training to specialize in cosmetic dermatologic surgery. For additional information, visit
abderm.org
.
Expand
American Board of Plastic Surgery
Also overseen by the ABMS
, the American Board of Plastic Surgery
certifies physicians to perform the complete array of cosmetic and reconstructive surgical procedures.
Physicians certified by this board have a medical degree from an accredited institution, at least two years of residency training in general surgery, and at least two years of additional training in plastic surgery.
For more information, visit
abplasticsurgery.org
.
Expand
Other organizations
If you’re planning to have a surgical procedure in the physician’s office,
ask whether the office is accredited by one of the following organizations:
• The American Association for Accreditation of Ambulatory Surgery Facilities
• The Accreditation Association for Ambulatory Health Care
• The Joint Commission.
Most of the organizations listed in the Resources section of this report offer referral services and can give you the names of physicians in your area.
Fizkes / iStock / Getty Images Plus
CHOOSING AN AESTHETICIAN
Expand
Cosmetology
Professional training in nonmedical skin care
used to come under the umbrella of cosmetology. Anyone who wanted to specialize in skin care also had to learn how to cut, color, and style hair and how to do nails.
Today, though, the health and medical aspects of skin care are recognized with specialized training and licensing for aestheticians. Below, learn about the aesthetician's role.
PS: "Aesthetician" can be a tongue-twister of a word to pronounce.
Click here
for Merriam-Webster's audio pronunciation guide to the word.
Renphoto / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
What aestheticians do
Aestheticians perform a variety of skin care procedures
—deep cleansing, facials, low-grade chemical peels, and microdermabrasion. (True dermabrasion should be performed only by an experienced physician.)
While most aestheticians work in salons and spas, don’t be surprised if your dermatologist or cosmetic surgeon has an aesthetician on staff.
In addition to performing noninvasive treatments, an aesthetician may assist your doctor in pre- and post-procedure skin care and teach you makeup techniques to cover redness or scars.
Nikodash / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
Licensing requirements
Licensing requirements for aestheticians vary from state to state,
ranging from 350 to 1,500 hours of classroom time and supervised time treating patients.
Contact your state’s department of licensing and regulation to learn about licensing requirements in your area. Many states offer this information online.
EtiAmmos / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
Words of warning
Most of the techniques described in this section
are available at both dermatologists’ offices and “medspas”—nonmedical facilities that offer a range of cosmetic services. However, in some spas and similar offices, people who are not well trained or supervised do some of the treatments. This can be dangerous.
A study published in
JAMA Dermatology
found that the percentage of lawsuits from skin laser surgery performed by nonphysicians more than doubled from 2008 to 2011, calling into question the safety of some medspas. There’s no reason to believe the situation has improved since then.
If you do decide to see an aesthetician instead of a dermatologist, make sure the practitioner is licensed in your state and is certified by the National Coalition of Estheticians, Manufacturers/Distributors and Associations (NCEA).
Aquir / iStock / Getty Images Plus

What aestheticians do
Aestheticians perform a variety of skin care procedures
—deep cleansing, facials, low-grade chemical peels, and microdermabrasion. (True dermabrasion should be performed only by an experienced physician.)
While most aestheticians work in salons and spas, don’t be surprised if your dermatologist or cosmetic surgeon has an aesthetician on staff.
In addition to performing noninvasive treatments, an aesthetician may assist your doctor in pre- and post-procedure skin care and teach you makeup techniques to cover redness or scars.
Nikodash / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
Licensing requirements
Licensing requirements for aestheticians vary from state to state,
ranging from 350 to 1,500 hours of classroom time and supervised time treating patients.
Contact your state’s department of licensing and regulation to learn about licensing requirements in your area. Many states offer this information online.
EtiAmmos / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
Words of warning
Most of the techniques described in this section
are available at both dermatologists’ offices and “medspas”—nonmedical facilities that offer a range of cosmetic services. However, in some spas and similar offices, people who are not well trained or supervised do some of the treatments. This can be dangerous.
A study published in
JAMA Dermatology
found that the percentage of lawsuits from skin laser surgery performed by nonphysicians more than doubled from 2008 to 2011, calling into question the safety of some medspas. There’s no reason to believe the situation has improved since then.
If you do decide to see an aesthetician instead of a dermatologist, make sure the practitioner is licensed in your state and is certified by the National Coalition of Estheticians, Manufacturers/Distributors and Associations (NCEA).
Aquir / iStock / Getty Images Plus

INJECTABLE TREATMENTS: BOTULINUM TOXIN
Injectable botulinum toxin therapies
have become extremely popular; Americans spent more than $1 billion on them in 2018. Learn more by clicking on the "Expand" button in each box below.
WHAT IT DOES AND WHERE IT HELPS
Botulinum toxin acts on dynamic wrinkles—
the lines etched by facial expressions such as laughing, smiling, frowning, wincing, squinting, and pursing your lips. It does not effectively treat nasolabial folds—those deep creases from nose to mouth. And sometimes a dynamic scowl line is too deep and entrenched to relax. In these cases, you may also need soft-tissue fillers to raise the depressed surface area left by the lines.
Botulinum toxin also doesn’t improve the appearance of
static wrinkles
, which form because of photoaging or chronological aging. Laser resurfacing does a better job on those.
TanyaLovus / iStock / Getty Images
BOTULINUM TOXIN PRIMER
Expand
What is botulinum toxin?﻿﻿
Since botulinum toxin injections were first used for cosmetic purposes in the late 1980s,
this therapy has gained quite a following. According to the American Society for Aesthetic Plastic Surgery, in 2022 (the most recent complete data), providers delivered about 4 million botulinum toxin injections, making it one of the leading nonsurgical cosmetic procedures in the United States among both sexes and all age groups.
In addition to Botox, the best-known brand, there are three similar brands of botulinum toxin injections—Dysport, Jeuveau, and Xeomin.
In 2022, the FDA approved a longer-acting product called daxibotulinumtoxinA-lanm (Daxxify), which lasts three to six months, compared with three to four months for the other products.
Prostock-Studio / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
Why is it so popular? ﻿
Wondering why
neuromodulator treatment
for wrinkles and frown lines is so popular?
Botulinum toxin injections
are relatively affordable, averaging $550 per treatment, according to the website Real Self (
realself.com
), which compiles reviews on aesthetic procedures. (Daxxify, because it is injected less often, is more expensive.)
Botox injections also:
• have few risks
• require no recovery time
• are effective at temporarily smoothing a wrinkled face, brow, or neck.
With continued use, the results tend to last longer, and the therapy inhibits the formation of deeper, more permanent facial lines over time.
Effects generally last four to six months. With continued use, the effects tend to last even longer, and the therapy inhibits the formation of deeper, more permanent facial lines over time.
Moussa81 / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
Where it comes from
Botulinum toxin type A
is a protein produced by the bacterium
Clostridium botulinum
.
If you ingest this bacterium in improperly preserved foods or if it infects a wound, it can cause botulism, a rare but potentially deadly disease.
But when tiny doses of sterile, purified botulinum toxin are injected into specific muscle sites, the solution doesn’t enter the bloodstream, and the procedure causes no harm. The amount used in a cosmetic treatment is far less than the amount necessary to cause illness.
Luismmolina / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
How it works
Botulinum toxin works by blocking the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine,
which helps trigger muscle movement. By blocking acetylcholine in a few strategic areas, botulinum toxin interferes with the ability of the selected muscles to contract, effectively immobilizing them. The muscles controlling facial expressions relax, and creases in the skin smooth out. Because the muscle can’t contract, new creases don’t form.
Botulinum toxin injections take just minutes and don’t cause much discomfort. You may notice mild redness for a few hours, a minor headache, or occasionally minor bruising, which you can camouflage with makeup. You should notice a change in your appearance in three to seven days.
Pictured: Botulinum toxin, which prevents the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine from axon endings at the neuromuscular junction.
ibreakstock /  iStock / Getty images
Expand
About the results
Many people worry
that botulinum toxin injections will leave them with an unnatural expression or with frozen or asymmetrical features. But when done well, botulinum toxin injections shouldn’t drastically change your ability to form facial expressions.
In rare cases, injections near the upper eyelids or eyebrows may make them droop temporarily. But side effects are typically uncommon and minimal.
Praetorianphoto / Getty Images E+
Expand
Words of warning
Safety with botulinum toxin is a real concern
when poorly trained, unlicensed practitioners deliver it. Experts warn that a “Botox party” in someone’s home or office is not an appropriate or safe way to receive a medical treatment, even a cosmetic one.
Beware also of websites selling botulinum toxin from unknown locations. These may be bogus products or simply a hoax to take your money.
Dynamic Graphics / Dynamic Graphics Group / Getty Images
Expand
Combination play
B
otulinum toxin is often used along with other cosmetic procedures.
Doctors sometimes recommend it with laser resurfacing, since skin heals better when it’s not in constant motion, or to enhance results with soft-tissue augmentation.
Botulinum toxin injections should be continued after a facelift or skin resurfacing, keeping the newly smooth face and skin from becoming creased again.
6okean / iStock / Getty Images

SKIN BASICS
There's more to your skin
than what you see on the surface. Click on the "Expand" button in each box below to learn about the various layers, plus a detail that might surprise you: Your hair and nails are actually skin, too.
LAYER BY LAYER
Expand
DID YOU KNOW?
Even at its thickest point,
our skin is only a few millimeters thick. But it's still the body's largest and heaviest organ, making up about 1/7th of our body weight. Depending on your height and body mass, your skin weighs between 7.5 and 22 pounds.
Expand
SPECIALIZED SKIN STRUCTURES
To carry out all of the functions
noted above, your skin relies on the specialized structures in its three layers:
• The epidermis
• The dermis
• The subcutaneous layer (sometimes called the hypodermis, or fat layer—as noted in the detail below).
Above: Harvard Health Publishing
Left: About Time / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
OUTERMOST LAYER
The epidermis—the outermost layer of the skin
—is a protective physical barrier, about as thick as a piece of paper. The very top portion of the epidermis is known as the stratum corneum. It’s composed of cells called keratinocytes that produce a tough protein called keratin, forming a flexible outer shield.
As the older cells on the surface of the epidermis die and fall off or are rubbed off, younger cells from below rise to take their place. This continuous cycle completely renews the skin surface about once a month.
The epidermis plays a key role in protecting you from the sun’s radiation.
In particular, pigmented cells called melanocytes are located at the bottom of the epidermis. These cells produce the melanin, or pigment, that colors skin and helps protect against ultraviolet radiation. When exposed to sunlight, the melanocytes churn out more melanin, and the skin darkens to help shield against further damage. If the melanocytes become cancerous, the condition is termed melanoma.
VolodymyrV / iStock / Getty Images Pl
us
Expand
THE MIDDLE LAYER
The dermis lies directly beneath the epidermis.
It is a thicker layer that contains collagen, blood and lymph vessels, nerves, hair follicles, and glands that produce sweat and oil.
• Blood vessels in the dermis expand or contract to maintain a constant body temperature.
• White blood cells patrol the dermis to fight infectious microbes that manage to break through the epidermis.
• Cells called fibroblasts secrete collagen, which gives the skin its strength and firmness.
• Elastin fibers made of protein in the dermis give skin its elasticity.
• Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a humectant—a substance that retains moisture —and it's capable of binding over one thousand times its weight in water. Hyaluronic acid is naturally found in many areas of the human body—not only the skin, but the eyes and the synovial fluid of the joints. (Note: HA used in beauty and skincare products is primarily made by bacteria in a lab via a process called biofermentation.)
Yomogi1 / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
THE DEEPEST LAYER
The subcutaneous tissue (hypodermis),
which consists of connective tissue and fat, lies between the dermis and underlying muscles or bones. It, too, contains blood vessels and infection-fighting white blood cells, but not to the same extent as in the dermis. Fat in the subcutaneous layer stores nutrients and insulates and cushions muscles and bones.
S
olar22
/ iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
HAIR AND NAILS
Your nails are skin, too.
They’re a thickened, hardened form of epidermis. Nail cells originate from the base of the nail bed. They die quickly, but unlike the keratinocytes, they aren’t sloughed off. They’re also composed of a much stronger form of keratin. Thus, a nail is simply a sheet of keratin like the topmost layer of skin, but much harder and thicker.
By contrast, a strand of hair, which is produced in a hair follicle, is a thin fiber made of many overlapping layers of keratin.
Above: Igor_kel/ iStock / Getty Images Plus
Left: Dr_Microbe / :iStock / Getty Images Plus

THREE KINDS OF SKIN CANCER
Those carefree days you spent sunning and swimming as a child
may have caused more damage than the lines and wrinkles of photoaging. They may have also set the stage for skin cancer. There are three types:
squamous cell carcinoma,
basal cell carcinoma
, and the most deadly,
melanoma
. Click on the "Expand" buttons in the boxes below to learn more.
SKIN CANCER TRENDS AND TYPES
Expand
HOW COMMON IS SKIN CANCER?
It's the most common cancer in the United States,
causing about 11,500 deaths annually. Most of these deaths (about 6,850) are from
melanoma
, the most severe form of skin cancer, which has become more common because people are spending more leisure time outdoors.
The other two types of skin cancer are
squamous cell carcinoma
and
basal cell carcinoma
. The good news is that with early detection and treatment, more than 90% of cases can be cured, although the results can be disfiguring. Doctors can also treat precancerous lesions called actinic keratoses, a subject covered later in this section.
The illustration at left
shows the relation of squamous cells, melanocytes, and basal cells in a healthy epidermis. Below is a 3D rendering of the three types of cancers and their depths into the epidermis and dermis.
Above: Naeblys / iStock / Getty Images
Left: ttsz / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMA
This type of cancer
begins in the middle portion of the epidermis and affects only the surrounding area, but it eventually forms a raised patch with a rough surface.
Harvard Health Publishing
Expand
BASAL CELL CARCINOMA
The cells of this type of cancer
resemble the cells in the lowest layer of the epidermis, the basal layer. The malignant cells invade surrounding tissues, forming a painless bump that later becomes an open ulcer with a hard edge.
Harvard Healthy Publishing
Expand
MALIGNANT MELANOMA
The deadliest form of skin cancer
occurs when melanocytes—pigment-making cells in the basal layer or in surface moles—begin reproducing uncontrollably. Left untreated, the cancer can spread to distant parts of the body.

THREE KINDS OF SKIN CANCER
Those carefree days you spent sunning and swimming as a child
may have caused more damage than the lines and wrinkles of photoaging. They may have also set the stage for skin cancer. There are three types:
squamous cell carcinoma,
basal cell carcinoma
, and the most deadly,
melanoma
. Click on the "Expand" buttons in the boxes below to learn more.
SKIN CANCER TRENDS AND TYPES
Expand
HOW COMMON IS SKIN CANCER?
It's the most common cancer in the United States,
causing about 11,500 deaths annually. Most of these deaths (about 6,850) are from
melanoma
, the most severe form of skin cancer, which has become more common because people are spending more leisure time outdoors.
The other two types of skin cancer are
squamous cell carcinoma
and
basal cell carcinoma
. The good news is that with early detection and treatment, more than 90% of cases can be cured, although the results can be disfiguring. Doctors can also treat precancerous lesions called actinic keratoses, a subject covered later in this section.
The illustration at left
shows the relation of squamous cells, melanocytes, and basal cells in a healthy epidermis. Below is a 3D rendering of the three types of cancers and their depths into the epidermis and dermis.
Above: Naeblys / iStock / Getty Images
Left: ttsz / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMA
This type of cancer
begins in the middle portion of the epidermis and affects only the surrounding area, but it eventually forms a raised patch with a rough surface.
Harvard Health Publishing
Expand
BASAL CELL CARCINOMA
The cells of this type of cancer
resemble the cells in the lowest layer of the epidermis, the basal layer. The malignant cells invade surrounding tissues, forming a painless bump that later becomes an open ulcer with a hard edge.
Harvard Healthy Publishing
Expand
MALIGNANT MELANOMA
The deadliest form of skin cancer
occurs when melanocytes—pigment-making cells in the basal layer or in surface moles—begin reproducing uncontrollably. Left untreated, the cancer can spread to distant parts of the body.
Harvard Health Publishing

CAN FAST SKIN CHECKS REALLY SPOT CANCERS?
If you’ve had a skin check by a dermatologist,
it may seem like he or she did the job in minutes—and couldn’t possibly have seen and evaluated all of your moles, skin tags, bumps, and other skin lesions. But after three years of skin-focused training during residency, dermatologists really are able to quickly identify suspicious lesions over most of the body in just minutes.
Kali9 / Getty Images E+
SCIENCE SHOWS . . .
A recent study from Belgium found that exams ranging from 41 to 232 seconds were effective for detecting skin cancers, while an American study found the duration of the typical total body exam ranges from 75 to 243 seconds.
The most frequently inspected sites, according to an international survey of 173 skin cancer specialists, are the scalp, ears, face and neck, armpit, trunk, breasts, arms and legs, palms and soles, fingernails and toenails, areas between the fingers and toes, and buttocks.
The genitals, particularly in women, were not as frequently inspected to avoid embarrassing people by checking an area where there is a low chance of skin cancers developing and which other specialists are likely to examine.
QUICK TIPS
To ease your worries that your doctor has done a thorough job, examine your skin yourself before you go in for an annual skin exam. (Just ahead: a lesson called "How to Do a Skin Self-Exam.") Then you can point out specific lesions that worry you to your doctor.
Also be sure to ask any specialists who deal with your genitals—a primary care doctor, gynecologist, urologist, or other doctor—to check the skin in those areas closely for potential cancers.
After three years of skin-focused training during residency, dermatologists really are able to quickly identify suspicious lesions over most of the body in just minutes.
35007 / Getty Images E+

MOISTURIZERS
A good moisturizer
is one of the foundations of an effective skin care regimen for dry, older skin. Moisturizers can soothe dry skin and make wrinkles less noticeable, even though the effect is temporary. But with so many to choose from, how do you decide?
Aja Koska / Getty Images E+
WHAT'S IN MOISTURIZERS?
Most moisturizers contain ingredients such as:
Water
Glycerin
(a compound made from vegetable oil or animal fat)
Petrolatum
(petroleum jelly)
Stearic acid
(a saturated fatty acid).
Many moisturizers contain the organic compound
propylene glycol
and
lanolin
, a wax, but some people are allergic to those ingredients, and they aren’t the best hydrators anyway. More desirable are
ceramides
, waxy lipids (fats) naturally found in the skin that maintain the skin barrier and help it retain moisture.
Some moisturizers contain
botanical ingredients
, such as jojoba oil, coconut oil, safflower oil, and linoleic acid, which help maintain the skin’s outer layer of keratin and keep skin supple.
Manufacturers use other ingredients, such as
cetyl alcohol
(a fatty alcohol),
palmitic acid
(a fatty acid), and
dimethicone
(a silicone), to give moisturizers a creamy, velvety, or translucent look and feel, and to help shore up the “mortar” that keeps skin soft and smooth.
OTHER INGREDIENTS:
Some moisturizers also contain sunscreens and cosmeceuticals, which help prevent or even correct fine wrinkles, uneven skin pigmentation, and other signs of aging and photoaging.

ULTRAVIOLET RAYS OF THE SUN
The single most important thing you can do to protect your skin
is to reduce sun exposure. That means using sunscreen, wearing protective clothing, and limiting your time outdoors when the sun is strongest.
Beyond that, there are steps you can take to manage the problems of aging skin, ranging from dryness to wrinkles. To keep your skin looking its best, take care to treat it gently, keep it hydrated, and report any changes you notice to your doctor immediately.
Grafxart8888 / iStock / Getty Images Plus
SKIN HAZARDS
While the sun’s ultraviolet (UV) rays
are by far the biggest factor leading to skin damage, several other factors can also contribute. Here’s a rundown of the key hazards, starting with sunlight, and what to do about them.
Sun damage
is responsible for photoaging, the leading cause of skin damage as you age. For enthusiasts of the great outdoors, sun protection is not an easy task. But careful selection and application of a sunscreen can go a long way toward preventing further skin damage.
It’s also important to wear a protective hat and other protective clothing when you’re out in the sun. Choose a hat with a wide brim that extends 2 to 3 inches to shade your neck, nose, and ears (a baseball cap doesn’t cut it). Also wear clothes with a tight weave, long sleeves, and long legs. Companies such as Coolibar, Solbari, and Sun Precautions make special sun-protective clothing.
But even the most scrupulous use of sunscreens and protective clothing is not as effective as minimizing exposure to dangerous UV light in the first place. The sun’s rays are most intense between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m., so if you’re going to be spending time outside, try to do so in the early morning or late afternoon. Stay in the shade when possible, and be aware of reflected light from water, sand, and (in winter) snow.
Don’t forget to look after the younger generation as well by protecting your children or grandchildren from sun exposure. Most sun damage that leads to skin cancer occurs early in life.
Ridofranz / iStock / Getty Images Plus
INFRARED RADIATION
A less common source of skin damage
is exposure to infrared radiation (heat). It’s not a major cause of skin damage, but researchers believe it may add to the effects of UV rays. Some exposures may occur on the job—think of the short-order cook who stands over a hot stove or in front of infrared heat lamps all day.
Infrared heat lamps also are used to heal some muscle injuries or to relieve pain and stiffness from osteoarthritis.

THREE-STEP DAILY SKIN-CARE PROGRAM
For daily skin care,
limit your regimen to three simple steps:
Cleaning
Protecting your skin from the sun
Tending to any specific skin problems you may have such as dry skin, acne, or fine lines and wrinkles.
Products for all three steps are available at reasonable costs from retail stores. Buying expensive lotions with exotic or pseudoscientific names will not produce better results.
1. CLEAN YOUR SKIN
1. CLEAN YOUR SKIN
Choose your skin cleanser
based on whether your skin is dry or oily:
• If you have dry skin,
choose a mild cleaning agent (such as Dove, Cetaphil, or Aveeno soaps or cleansers) and avoid products including toners that contain alcohol.
• For oily skin,
choose a soap that removes the oil. Facial blotting sheets are inexpensive and effective options for absorbing excess skin oil.
Maridav / iStock / Getty Images
2. PROTECT YOUR SKIN FROM SUN
2. PROTECT YOUR SKIN FROM SUN
Choose a broad-spectrum sunscreen
with an SPF (Sun Protection Factor) of 30 or higher and wear it every day. Higher SPFs are useful if you plan to spend hours outdoors, but if you spend most of your time indoors, SPF 30 is generally sufficient.
If you have skin that’s easily irritated, choose a sunscreen designed for sensitive skin.
Mykola Sosiukin / iStock / Getty Images
3. TREAT YOUR SKIN NEEDS
3. TREAT YOUR SKIN NEEDS
Think about your particular needs
for healthy skin. Three examples:
• For dry skin, effective and inexpensive moisturizers are available (see the lesson directly ahead).
• Interested in adult acne products? See our related lesson
by clicking here
.
• If you want to consider products that moderately reduce lines and wrinkles or fades brown spots, click ahead; the rest of this section is devoted to a variety of choices.
PeopleImages / Getty Images E+

ATHLETE'S FOOT: HOW TO TREAT IT AND PREVENT IT
Athlete’s foot, or
tinea pedis
, is a common foot infection,
one that can produce almost unbearable itching. Click on the photographs below to learn about causes, treatment, and prevention.
SIGNS AND SYMPTOMS
WHAT CAUSES ATHLETE'S FOOT?
Athlete's foot is caused
by group of fungi on the surface of the skin known as dermatophytes (a name derived from the Greek words for “skin” and “plant”). Found in many different places indoors and outdoors, dermatophytes are especially common in the warm, moist environments of pools, showers, locker rooms, and other sports facilities, where people walk with bare feet.
Once dermatophytes contaminate the skin of a foot, the warm, moist environment of sweaty socks and shoes encourages them to grow.
BonNontawat / iStock / Getty Images
TREATMENT
TREATMENT
If you think you have athlete’s foot,
first try a nonprescription antifungal ointment, cream, or powder, such as
clotrimazole
(Lotrimin AF, Mycelex, generic),
terbinafine
(Lamisil AT, Silka, generic), or
miconazole
(Lotrimin AF spray, Micatin, generic). It can take weeks for an infection to improve, and recurrences are common.
If your symptoms don’t improve after several weeks, consult a doctor, who may prescribe an oral antifungal medicine, such as
itraconazole
(Sporanox, generic) or
terbinafine
(Lamisil, generic).
Verbaska_studio / iStock Editorial / Getty Images
PREVENTION: HYGIENE
PREVENTION: HYGIENE
You can help to prevent athlete’s foot
by keeping your feet clean and dry. More specifically, you can try the following.
•  Wash your feet well every day and wear a clean pair of socks after your bath or shower.
• Take time to dry your feet thoroughly (including each toe and especially the web space between the toes) after you bathe, shower, or swim.
• If you use public pools or showers, wear flip-flops or sandals to prevent your bare feet from touching floors contaminated with fungi.
PREVENTION: FOOTWEAR
PREVENTION: FOOTWEAR
Follow these easy guidelines
to help keep athlete's foot away.
• Choose shoes that let your feet “breathe” so they are more likely to stay dry.
• Wear moisture-wicking socks to absorb sweat.
• If possible, don’t wear the same pair of shoes two days in a row. Give shoes a 24-hour break between wearings to air out and dry out.
• Don’t share shoes.
waddle / iStock / Getty Images Pluss
ATHLETE'S FOOT
SIGNS AND SYMPTOMS
These are the typical signs of athlete's foot:
• Intense itching of the feet
• Cracked, blistered, or peeling areas of skin, especially between the toes
• Redness and scaling on the soles.
Carroteater / iStock / Getty Images

TYPES OF COSMETIC TREATMENTS
Beauty may only be skin deep,
but in a culture that values youth, having young-looking skin can boost your confidence and self-image. Today, a host of cosmetic procedures can help improve your appearance, reducing the effects of time and sun exposure—within limits.
Prostock-Studio / iStock / Getty Images Plus
KEEP IN MIND
C
osmetic treatments and technology
are getting better all the time, but remember that the goal is improvement, not perfection. Skin continues aging, whether you treat it or not, so no single procedure will restore youthful skin for good.
Michaeljung / iStock / Getty Images
PRIMER ON PROCEDURES
Expand
WHAT TREATMENTS CAN DO
Realistically, nothing will restore the skin you had in your 20s.
But powerful lasers, injectable wrinkle fillers, and other recent technologies can remove fine lines, fill in deeper ones, firm up jawlines, and lighten pigmented areas or spots.
These procedures help rejuvenate skin, with few side effects and far less recovery time (and expense) than what would be required for surgical techniques such as facelifts.
Expand
TRENDS IN TREATMENT
Not long ago,
the procedures that predominated were deep chemical peels and full laser resurfacing, both of which are harsh but effective. Now the trend is toward less invasive techniques that don’t wound the skin as extensively.
Recovery time—how long it takes for skin to heal and return to normal color and texture—is one of the most important factors for people seeking such cosmetic procedures. Most people want to return to work and social activities as quickly as possible.
The trade-off is that the effects are not as significant, and a series of treatments and “maintenance” visits is often necessary. However, with today’s more sophisticated laser treatments, it's now possible to find a happy medium: moderate effects with minimal downtime.
Expand
FOUR MAIN TECHNIQUES
The techniques described in this section
fall into four general categories:
Injectable treatments that smooth and fill wrinkles
Skin resurfacing techniques that target fine lines, age spots, and skin discoloration
Skin tightening or lifting procedures that smooth and tone skin, especially around the eyes, jaw, and neckline
Light-based treatments using LED or other lights to tone the skin.
Expand
COMBINATION TREATMENTS﻿﻿
The four techniques described above may be used alone
but increasingly they're used in combination (or with other cosmetic techniques) to treat deeper wrinkles or scars on one area of the face, for example,  and finer lines or superficial lesions on another.
In fact, clinicians often recommend a combination of procedures to achieve a desired effect. Example: For an older woman seeking more pronounced skin tightening (and willing to put up with some downtime), a doctor might recommend:
•
B
otulinum toxin
for frown lines and forehead lines
•
F
illers
for nasolabial folds
•
Q-switched laser or intense pulsed light treatments
to remove freckling and diminish redness
•
F
ractional laser resurfacing
for all-over improvement.
In the lesson directly ahead, you'll find a downloadable and printable list of the most common skin problems and available treatments. The lessons in the rest of this section describe skin therapies in greater detail.

QUESTIONS TO ASK YOUR DOCTOR
Before undergoing any cosmetic procedure
that requires a medical professional—such as botulinum toxin injections, filler injections, or laser resurfacing—ask how many procedures of this type the clinician performs per month or year. Find out also how long he or she has been doing this procedure. Expertise levels can vary, but you want someone who has had specialized training—perhaps a fellowship in an academic medical center, rather than just a course or two.
Many clinicians provide the opportunity to come in for a consultation appointment prior to scheduling any procedures, which, among other things, gives you the opportunity to decide whether he or she seems like a good fit for your needs.
sjenner13 / iStock / Getty Images Plus
QUERIES TO CONSIDER
You should also ask about cost, since cosmetic procedures are not likely to be covered by insurance. For example:
Is there a consultation fee?
If so, can it be deducted from the cost of the procedure?
What is the complete cost for the procedure, including the physician’s fee, anesthesiology, and operating-room costs?
Who delivers the anesthesia?
Are there extra charges for corrections or repairs?
Also, make sure you understand the complications that could occur and exactly what is involved in recovery. The healing process for some procedures can be lengthy and tedious.
Another important consideration is whether the procedure will need to be repeated and how often. Some of the less invasive procedures have shorter recovery times, but require several treatments or even periodic maintenance procedures every few months.
AND FINALLY . . .
Don’t forget to trust your gut. Choose a physician with whom you feel comfortable and can establish an easy rapport.
It’s helpful, too, if the physician has a courteous, knowledgeable staff. Finally, keep in mind that no matter how talented or experienced your physician is, no amount of filling, lasering, or peeling is going to make you look 20 years old again.
Don’t forget to trust your gut. Choose a physician with whom you feel comfortable and can establish an easy rapport.

INJECTABLE TREATMENTS: SOFT-TISSUE FILLERS
Soft-tissue injectable fillers
are considered to be a relatively safe cosmetic procedure, but it’s important to choose an experienced doctor to perform the injections. Click on each photographto learn more.
WHAT INJECTABLE FILLERS DO
WHAT INJECTABLE FILLERS DO
Soft-tissue fillers are i
njected under the skin
for a number of reasons:
• To add height to cheekbones
• To improve the jawline
• To diminish acne or surgical scars
• To restore fullness to hollow cheeks and eyes
• To fill vertical fine lines
• To resculpt lips that have been thinned by sun exposure or smoking.
Injectable fillers also can fill in nasolabial folds, the deep lines that run from the outside of the nostrils to the corners of the mouth that virtually no other skin rejuvenation procedure can correct. This technique minimizes deep lines, wrinkles, and grooves by lifting the surrounding skin and concealing surface imperfections. Fillers are both beauty enhancers and buffers against telltale signs of aging.
SOFT-TISSUE ATROPHY
UNDERSTANDING "SOFT-TISSUE ATROPHY"
In the process of aging,
many people lose collagen and fat under the skin. This process is called soft-tissue atrophy.
Soft-tissue loss commonly affects the lips and corners of the mouth, the chin, the cheeks, the areas under and between the eyes, and the nasolabial folds. As these areas become less plump, they fold more easily.
Injections of soft-tissue fillers can often remedy this. These procedures are popular because they can accentuate the positive and minimize the negative in your face. They aren’t as invasive or costly as a facelift, and they don’t require much recovery time.
skynesher  Getty Images
WHICH FILLER?
WHICH FILLER?
Injectable fillers come in many different concentrations and formulations
designed to remedy lines and wrinkles of different depths. With so many old and new selections available, the task of deciding which filler might suit your needs has become a complicated process.
Do you want something temporary that has a very low risk? Or would you prefer something more permanent, even if it has a higher complication rate? Are you willing to try a new product? Or do you want something that has a proven track record?
Elena Volf / iStock / Getty images
INJECTION BASICS
INJECTION BASICS
You’ll likely receive soft-filler tissue injections at several sites.
Usually, the doctor inserts tiny amounts in the skin, carefully layering the material up to the desired fullness. He or she then gently massages the area to spread the material evenly.
A session typically takes less than an hour. Costs vary with the material used. The average cost of hyaluronic acid dermal fillers is $715, the average cost of non-hyaluronic acid dermal fillers is $901 and the average cost of lip augmentation with dermal fillers is $743, according to the latest statistics from the American Society of Plastic Surgeons
You’ll need very little follow-up care, other than ice during the first few hours. But for most fillers, you'll need follow-up injections if you want to maintain the effect.
Zoranm / Getty Images
ASK YOUR DOCTOR
ASK YOUR DOCTOR
If you're considering
a soft-tissue filler, make sure you're comfortable with the procedure. Here are some questions to ask your doctor:
• How long have you used this product or technique? How often do you use it?
• How widely used is this product or technique? If it is new or uncommon, why do you think it would be substantially better for my needs than other products?
• What material is the filler or implant? Is it natural or synthetic?
• How often do you see complications or side effects?
• Are the results of the treatment temporary, long-lasting, or permanent?
• If the procedure is permanent, can I have it modified or removed?
Lordn / iStock / Getty Images Plus
POSSIBLE SIDE EFFECTS
POSSIBLE SIDE EFFECTS
Side effects associated with fillers
injected into the lips and cheeks may include noticeable, sustained swelling and overfilling (think of the lips of some aging celebrities who have obviously had fillers injected and look unnatural).
Attempts to reshape the nose with fillers have been associated with more serious but rare side effects, such as blindness and tissue death.
Lordn / iStock / Getty Images Plus

YOUR SKIN'S FUNCTIONS
Your skin
is much more than a simple covering.
It's a vibrant, dynamic organ that responds to environmental signals in a variety of ways. As described by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), our skin "is a stable but flexible outer covering that acts as barrier, protecting your body from harmful things in the outside world such as moisture, the cold and sun rays, as well as germs and toxic substances."

HANDWASHING IN THE AGE OF COVID-19
As COVID-19 began to sweep the country in early 2020,
people who never thought too much about proper handwashing suddenly became much more diligent. Here’s how to do the job right—and also keep the skin on your hands from drying out.
HOW TO WASH
HOW TO WASH
Use soap and lukewarm water (turn off the water while you’re soaping) and clean for 20 seconds. Be sure to include the backs of your hands and the areas between your fingers and around your nails. Many experts recommend singing the song “Happy Birthday” twice to be sure you’re washing for long enough. Then rinse.
Maurian Soares SalvadorCreative #:1214582435License type:Royalty-freeCollection:iStock / Getty Images Plus
WHEN TO WASH
WHEN TO WASH
Reach for the soap as soon as you come home from being outside and especially after shopping; after using the bathroom; after sneezing, coughing, or blowing your nose; before preparing food and before eating; and after touching pets.
Ryan McVay / Getty Images
HOW TO MOISTURIZE
HOW TO MOISTURIZE
Pat your hands dry and rub on a pea-sized amount of a thick hand cream, salve, or ointment such as Cetaphil or CeraVe while your hands are still slightly moist. Products that come in a tube or jar tend to be more therapeutic than thinner lotions that you pump out of a bottle. Avoid moisturizers that contain fragrances or dyes; they can irritate dry skin.
Also apply hand cream after you use hand sanitizer, which can be drying. If the skin on your hands is very dry or cracking, lubricate the skin while you sleep: apply petrolatum or a thick moisturizer like Neutrogena Norwegian Hand Formula before bed and slip on a pair of cloth gloves.
4FR / Getty Images E+

ACTINIC KERATOSES (AK)
An actinic keratosis (plural: keratoses)
can cause discomfort and itching, but its biggest danger is as a precursor to squamous cell carcinoma. Doctors can use several different methods to remove AKs. Most treatments can remove AKs without scarring. Learn more about AK treatments by clicking on the 'Expand" buttons below.
WHAT IS ACTINIC KERATOSIS?
Often abbreviated as "AK,"
actinic keratosis refers to a precancerous skin lesion that appears as a scaly pink or red-brown raised, rough patch on sun-exposed skin. Fair-skinned people are more likely than darker-complexioned individuals to develop AKs.
Harvard Health Publishing
AK TREATMENT
Expand
CRYOTHERAPY
The standard treatment for AKs is to freeze the area with liquid nitrogen.
Inflammation, swelling, and occasionally blistering may occur. A small scab forms, and healthy new skin emerges as it heals. Cryotherapy can be used to treat a specific lesion or multiple lesions.
CasarsaGuru / Getty Images :E+
Expand
CREAMS AND GELS
The prescription gel ingenol mebutate
(Picato) can be applied for three consecutive days to remove AKs.
Alternatively, a prescription cream containing the chemotherapy drug
fluorouracil
—also called
5-FU
(Carac, Efudex, Fluoroplex)—can be used once or twice a day for several weeks or on two consecutive days a week for several months.
Another topically applied drug, called
imiquimod
(Aldara, Zyclara, generic), is also very effective in treating AKs. Imiquimod stimulates the immune system to release cytokines, chemicals that fight cancer cells and viruses. (Pictured: 3D rendering of a cancer cell, left, surrounded by cytokines.)
With imiquimod treatment, AKs become inflamed, crust over, and heal.
All of these products cause an inflammatory reaction in the skin, resulting in temporary redness and discomfort. In addition, products containing fluorouracil cause sun sensitivity. In contrast, a topical gel called
diclofenac
(Solaraze, generic) does not cause redness, but requires months of treatment.
Marcin Klapczynski / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
PHOTODYNAMIC THERAPY
Photodynamic therapy
uses light to activate a therapeutic agent. In this case, the agent is
Levulan
, a topical solution of aminolevulinic acid—an acid that occurs naturally in the body to process heme, a component of red blood cells.
First, Levulan is applied to the spots, where it changes into a light-sensitive molecule. Several hours to a day later, light or laser treatment activates the drug and destroys the damaged cells. (Pictured: photodynamic therapy treating skin cancer on a patient's leg.)
In a few days, the spots crust over and heal. Photodynamic therapy is used in cases where someone has many AKs and sizable areas of the face and scalp need to be treated.
RapidEye / Getty Images E+
Expand
DEEPER TREATMENTS
Widespread facial patches
may warrant deeper treatments such as laser resurfacing with a carbon dioxide, erbium:YAG, or fractional 1927 nm laser or a strong chemical peel containing trichloroacetic acid, which dissolves the outer layers of the skin with a chemical solution.
Laser resurfacing and (pictured) chemical peels can involve local or general anesthesia. Depending on how deeply the skin is penetrated, they may cause considerable swelling.
Yet another effective treatment is curettage with electrodesiccation, which entails scraping away damaged tissue and using an electric probe to kill any remaining precancerous cells.
RobertoDavid / iStock / Getty Images

MELANOMA DRUG TREATMENTS
In the slideshow presentation you just watched,
we discussed melanoma signs, symptoms, diagnosis, and treatments. Among the treatments you learned about were surgery, immunotherapy (which strengthens the immune system against the cancer), chemotherapy, and radiation therapy. Another treatment involves medication. Click on the "Expand" button in each box to learn more.
PROGNOSIS
About half of people diagnosed with melanoma
have stage 0 or 1 or localized melanoma, meaning the disease has not spread beyond its original site. For them, the five-year survival rate is about 99%. But if the cancer spreads to the lymph nodes (stage 3), the five-year survival drops to around 65%.
As with most other forms of cancer, if the tumor has spread to distant organs (stage 4), overall survival is still lower—about 25%. The earlier melanoma is detected and treated, the better the outcome.
Inside Creative House / iStock / Getty Images
PRIMER: MELANOMA MEDICATIONS
Expand
IMMUNE CHECKPOINT INHIBITORS
Certain drugs can be used in combination
with other treatments to stop the progression of melanoma and extend survival by months or even years. For example,
immune checkpoint inhibitors
are used for people with advanced disease. Examples:
• pembrolizumab (Keytruda)
• nivolumab (Opdivo)
• ipilimumab (Yervoy)
These drugs remove proteins that would otherwise hinder the immune system from clearing melanoma cells.
Researchers are studying whether these drugs can be given before or after melanoma surgery to prevent the cancer from recurring.
Expand
TARGETED TREATMENTS
Newer, so-called "targeted treatments"
home in on specific genetic changes (mutations) seen in people with certain forms of melanoma. For example, about half of melanomas have mutations in a gene called BRAF, which signals melanoma cells to grow and divide quickly.
Drugs that inhibit BRAF
are now available to shrink melanomas. Examples:
• dabrafenib (Tafinlar)
• encorafenib (Braftovi)
• vemurafenib (Zelboraf).
Also available to shrink melanomas
are drugs that target related proteins called MEK proteins. Examples:
• binimetinib (Mektovi)
• cobimetinib (Cotellic)
• trametinib (Mekinist).
Expand
SIDE EFFECTS OF TARGETED TREATMENTS
Targeted treatments may have fewer side effects than chemotherapy drug
s
and work when chemotherapy doesn’t. On the downside, they may work for only a short period of time and then stop being effective, although they may work longer if they’re used in combination.
Another genetic defect, in a gene called C-KIT, is associated with a small number of melanomas. The drugs imatinib (Gleevec) and nilotinib (Tasigna), approved for use in other cancers, may be effective against C-KIT changes.
Axitinib (Inlyta), everolimus (Afinitor), and pazopanib (Votrient) are being studied in combination with other drugs for their ability to target other defective genes and proteins.
Expand
IN DEVELOPMENT: MELANOMA-PREVENTION VACCINES
Another exciting advance
is in the area of vaccines. Several melanoma vaccines—designed both to prevent melanoma and to treat existing cases—are now being tested in clinical trials.

examine your skin all over your body and watch for changes over time. By checking your skin regularly, you’ll learn what is normal for you. The best time to check your skin is after a shower or bath. EXFOLIANTS
Moisturizers that contain exfoliant ingredients
can improve the appearance of the skin by removing dead surface skin cells. As a result, they can smooth the skin’s appearance and even out some discoloration from too much sun exposure. Exfoliants can be particularly useful for aging skin that appears rough and sallow, because older skin doesn’t slough off dead surface cells as easily as younger skin does.
Two chemical exfoliants, alpha hydroxy acids (AHAs) and beta hydroxy acids (BHAs), are considered superior to many exfoliating scrubs, masks, soaps, toners, and abrasive cloths. That’s because they change cell growth patterns and may help renew collagen.
HconQ / iStock / Getty Images
A
LPHA HYDROXY ACIDS
Alpha hydroxy acids
(AHAs) are obtained from various fruits, including grapes, citrus fruits, and apples. You’ll find them listed on product labels as glycolic acid, lactic acid, malic acid, hydroxycaprylic acid, alpha-hydroxyoctanoic acid, triple fruit acid, or sugar cane extract.
Although the FDA does not regulate AHAs as drugs, it has issued guidelines on their safe use because they can cause skin irritation and increase skin sensitivity to UV rays.
For at-home use, it’s best to stick to products that contain an AHA concentration of 10% or less and have a pH of 3.5 or more (lower pH numbers are more acidic). You may or may not be instructed to rinse the product off. Whatever the product, though, users are advised to use sunscreen as well.
Many cosmetologists and skin aestheticians, who are licensed on a state-by-state basis, offer glycolic acid and lactic acid chemical peels at higher concentrations—but no greater than 30% with a pH no lower than 3.0—for brief skin care sessions provided that thorough rinsing and daily sun protection follow. In higher concentrations, AHAs should be applied by a physician (
click here
to see our lesson covering chemical peels).
BETA HYDROXY ACIDS
Beta hydroxy acids
(BHAs), another type of cosmetic exfoliant, are moderately effective at improving the surface of the skin. The BHA used in skin-care products is salicylic acid, a relative of aspirin. Salicylic acid is more effective than AHAs in reaching oily areas of the skin such as the pores, which makes it useful if you have oily skin or if you’re having problems with acne.
Salicylic acid can be found in many acne products at effective levels of 1.5% to 2%. But antiwrinkle products containing these ingredients tend not to list the percentage on their labels, so it’s difficult to know whether they contain sufficient amounts to be effective. The FDA recommends that you first test a BHA product on a small patch of skin to see if irritation occurs and that you use a sunscreen at the same time because BHAs can increase the skin’s sun sensitivity.
Exfoliants can be particularly useful for aging skin that appears rough and sallow, because older skin doesn’t slough off dead surface cells as easily as younger skin does.

RETINOIDS
Topical vitamin A-based drugs called retinoids
—the most widely used and best studied anti-aging compounds—may reduce fine lines and wrinkles. Tretinoin, under the brand name Retin-A, was the first retinoid. It was used as an acne treatment in the 1970s, but researchers later discovered it also fades sun spots, evens pigmentation, and speeds the turnover of superficial skin cells.
John Sommer / Getty Images
HOW RETINOIDS WORK
Retinoids reduce fine lines and wrinkles by increasing the production of collagen. They also stimulate the production of new blood vessels in the skin, which improves skin color. Additional benefits include fading age spots and softening rough patches of skin.
However, it takes three to six months of regular use before improvements in wrinkles are apparent. The best results take six to 12 months. Because retinoids can cause skin dryness and irritation, doctors often recommend using them only every other day at first and then gradually working up to nightly applications. Wear a sunscreen during the day, because retinoids increase the skin’s sensitivity to sunlight. These drugs must be used continually to maintain their benefits.
Prescription retinoids include:
tretinoin (Altreno, Retin-A, Renova, generic)
tazarotene (Avage, Tazorac)
trifarotene (Aklief)
adapalene (Differin).
Adapalene is also available over the counter (in a mild 0.1% formulation versus the 0.3% prescription version).
In addition, several over-the-counter products containing retinoids, such as Retinol, are available. Because they’re not as strong (and thus less irritating), they are not as effective in reducing wrinkles as tretinoin, but they do improve the appearance of photoaged skin. Tretinoin can be used with AHAs for additional skin-smoothing effects.
OTC HELP
Over-the-counter products containing retinoids
include Retinol. They’re not as strong and thus not as effective in reducing wrinkles as prescription retinoids like tretinoin, but they do improve the effects of photoaging. Plus, they're less irritating.

HYALURONIC ACID
Hyaluronic acid is a naturally occurring sugar
found in skin and tissues that can absorb 1,000 times its weight in water. Manufacturers claim it will plump up the skin and make it look more youthful.
Bravekanyawe / iStock / Getty Images Plus
AS AN INGREDIENT
Because it is so hydrating,
hyaluronic acid is a popular ingredient in all manner of skin-care serums and creams. The molecules are too large to penetrate deeply, but when applied topically, these products temporarily hydrate the skin’s surface by pulling water into the top layer.
Hyaluronic acid is also used in soft-tissue fillers. The effects last longer when it is injected into the deeper layers of the skin.
Bravekanyawe / iStock / Getty Images Plus
DOES IT WORK?
Is topical HA worth the hype?
In an article for
Harvard Health Publishing
, Kristina Liu, MD, MHS, and Janelle Nassim, MD, write, "First, let’s establish that topical HA will never be as effective as an injectable HA filler for replacing lost volume, even though some topical HA products are misleadingly marketed as topical 'fillers.' Topical HA is an excellent moisturizer. However, if the goal is to improve volume loss and laxity of the skin that naturally occurs with aging, injectable HA, rather than topical HA, is the preferred treatment method."
They also note that topical HA has multiple positive attributes: "It is generally well tolerated; it does not frequently cause allergic reactions or irritate sensitive skin; and it is safe to use on the skin during pregnancy and while breastfeeding. For individuals with dry skin, or for those who crave a more dewy, moisturized feel to their skin during the cooler months, a serum or moisturizer containing HA can be a great choice. But keep in mind that topical HA will provide hydration on a surface level, not deep within the skin."
HOW IT WORKS
Hyaluronic acid is a humectant
—a substance that retains moisture—and it is capable of binding over 1,000 times its weight in water. This substance is naturally found in many areas of the human body, including the skin, eyes, and synovial fluid of the joints. For use in beauty and skin-care products, HA is primarily made by bacteria in a lab via a process called biofermentation.
Ayo888 / iStock

CLEAN AND NATURAL PRODUCTS
The  $1.6 billion natural skin care market is hot today.
It’s propelled by cosmetic retailers, social influencers, bloggers, and celebrity endorsers like Gwyneth Paltrow on her Goop website. The pitch for so-called “clean beauty products” typically touts botanical ingredients, with no synthetic preservatives. But are they  as safe and effective as marketers claim?
CarlosGaw / Getty Images E+
ASSESSING BOTANICAL BOASTS
Extracts from plants such as aloe, seaweed, fruits, or herbs
can have a variety of effects on the skin. Aloe vera gel, which is extracted from the fleshy leaves of the aloe vera plant, is commonly used for burns, sunburn, frostbite, psoriasis, and cold sores. Other plant-based ingredients—such as alpha hydroxy acids, soy, and mushroom—might reduce or prevent wrinkles, but so far the evidence comes from small studies and animal research.
Overall, plant-based products are generally safe to use topically, but you should understand that there is little evidence regarding their effectiveness . And keep in mind that they can cause skin reactions in certain people.
RESEARCH GUIDANCE
In the publication
JAMA Dermatology,
an editorial in 2019 gives us some guidance. The article is titled “Natural Does Not Mean Safe — The Dirt on Clean Beauty Products.” Within the piece, two dermatologists at the University of Pennsylvania cautioned that misinformation from non-dermatologists is leading to high rates of contact dermatitis. The culprits are actually the very ingredients that consumers are being encouraged to favor—plant extracts.
Consider also a study of 241 men and women using natural products containing botanical ingredients like aloe, marigold, chamomile, propolis, and arnica. Results showed that 6% reported one or more skin reactions.
What’s more, unsubstantiated criticisms of well-established and safe ingredients are making people fearful and anxious about using products that do not pose a danger. Examples include
petrolatum
(a superior moisturizer that is unlikely to cause skin reactions) and
parabens
(preservatives that are nonallergenic and prevent infections of the cornea from mascara)  .
IMPORTANCE OF DOING YOUR HOMEWORK
So before you automatically reach for a natural product,
research the claims on the label and look for clinical trials to support the use of the individual ingredients it contains. If you decide to buy a product, be on the lookout for any unusual reactions, just as you would for products with synthetic ingredients. Since the FDA doesn’t regulate skin-care products, it’s up to you to be your own best beauty adviser.
Another point to remember: Information about personal care products should come from skin experts, not celebrities. Because everyone has different skin concerns, ask your dermatologist what types of products would be best for your skin specifically.
Information about personal care products should come from skin experts, not celebrities.

MEDICATIONS THAT CAN CAUSE PHOTOSENSITIVITY
If you’re taking certain medications,
sun exposure can cause additional damage to your skin. Such drugs, called photosensitizers, increase your sensitivity to UV radiation. Reactions include overly reddened skin, hives, swelling, and itchy, scaly skin. Cataracts and damage to blood vessels or the immune system also may occur.
Some of these drugs, such as heart medications, are more commonly taken by people as they get older. It’s important to check with your doctor to determine whether any of the drugs you’re taking could cause photosensitivity.

TAKING AIM AT DOUBLE CHIN PROBLEMS
In researching double chin treatments,
you may see the phrase "submental fullness," which can be genetic or caused by weight gain or simply by aging. In order to improve one's chin profile, people are turning to the two treatments described here. CoolSculpting (cryolipolysis) and fat-damaging injections (Kybella) are FDA-cleared options for submental fullness as well as submandibular (under the jawline) areas.
Click on the photographs to learn more.
CRYOLIPOLYSIS: "COOL MINI"
CRYOLIPOLYSIS: "COOL MINI"
This procedure, also known as “cool sculpting,"
treats a double chin. In fact, CoolSculpting is the trade name for cryolipolysis, a treatment that uses cold temperatures to destroy fat cells while avoiding frostbite to the overlying skin. It's based on a discovery that fat tissue can be damaged by cooling it to temperatures that do not harm surrounding skin, nerves, blood vessels, or muscle tissue. The procedure targets areas of fat that are resistant to diet or exercise, offering a noninvasive alternative to liposuction.
The clinician places a handpiece under the chin and chills fat cells there almost to the point of freezing, damaging their membranes so they're absorbed and metabolized by the body.
The treatment itself takes about an hour, and results are seen over the subsequent two months. Cryolipolysis is quite effective and can be performed more than once. It also can be used to reduce love handles on the waist.
The cost is approximately $1,225 per treatment.
This photo shows cryolipolysis, which involves the use of a "cold applicator" (which resembles a canister vacuum attachment) to cool the targeted area to between 30° and 40° F. The canister maintains that temperature for an hour. The technician then massages the cooled tissue for several minutes. The dead fat cells are absorbed by the body over the next several weeks.
Left: iStock / Getty Images Plus
Above: Courtesy Harvard Health Publishing
KYBELLA
: INJECTIONS TO DAMAGE FAT
KYBELLA:
INJECTIONS TO DAMAGE FAT
Approved by the FDA in 2016,
Kybella contains a substance that damages fat cells. After applying local or topical anesthesia, the physician selects 10 to 15 injection sites in the neck area.
The shots can be slightly uncomfortable, and there is potential for swelling, pain, and redness for one week. The fat under the chin will disappear over the course of the next four to six weeks.
Like cryolipolysis, these injections are very effective and can be repeated two or three times. The cost is about $1,350.
DOUBLE CHIN TREATMENTS
Many people are bothered by double chins
and try to conceal them behind turtleneck shirts and scarves. However, recent advances have expanded the options for jowl removal beyond surgery to in-office treatments.

SKIN RESURFACING, PART 1
Skin resurfacing techniques use different strategies.
Among them are chemicals that dissolve skin cells, tools that gently polish the skin’s surface, and targeted, intense beams of light (lasers) to improve the skin’s appearance, reducing wrinkles, improving skin tone and texture, and in some cases, tightening skin.
In this lesson, you'll find descriptions of several techniques: chemical peels, dermaplaning, microdermabrasion, and microneedling. Click on the "Expand" button in each box to learn more.
In the lesson following this one, you'll learn about: laser resurfacing, fractional laser resurfacing, and nonfractional ablative laser resurfacing.
CHEMICAL PEELS
Expand
Chemical peels explained
Chemical peels were once the first choice
for facial rejuvenation, photodamage, fine lines, and pigmentation. While they're now second fiddle to newer, less invasive techniques, chemical peels still have a significant role. They're used to treat wrinkles, age spots, discoloration, precancerous skin growths, and superficial scarring.
In performing a chemical peel,
the clinician applies an acid solution to the skin, dissolving skin cells and removing the top layers of the epidermis. Just how deeply the peel penetrates—and the effects—vary depending on the type and strength of the solution used.
Chemical peels can be done on the hands as well as the face. There is an abundance of research supporting the usefulness of these treatments, which can be done in a doctor’s office or a salon.
Elena Volf /
iStock / Getty Images
Expand
Recovery time and side effects
Recovery time for a chemical peel varies.
Some solutions may leave only mild redness for a few days, while others produce more extreme redness and peeling that needs to be carefully bandaged and takes weeks to heal.
The most commonly used cosmetic peels contain glycolic or salicylic acid. These mild peels have no permanent effects on the skin, but temporarily improve its appearance and texture and can be repeated intermittently.
For more advanced sun damage or if you want a more noticeable improvement to mild wrinkles, skin tone, and coloring, you may consider a stronger therapeutic or “deep” peel. These peels dissolve the skin into the top layer of dermis and require a longer recovery time. A solution of 35% to 50% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) is commonly used. Afterward, you’ll have mild discomfort, and a few days to a week after the treatment, the superficial skin will darken, turn stiff, and peel off. Your skin may appear flushed for several weeks. Results last up to two years, but many people repeat the treatment annually.
Side effects
of deep chemical peels include redness, itching, peeling, increased skin sensitivity, altered pigmentation, and sometimes fragile and blistering skin. Infection is rare. Allergic contact dermatitis can occur, and if your peel uses glycolic acid, irritant contact dermatitis is possible.
Any peel can irritate your skin, especially if the concentration of chemicals is too high or you use them too often. Also, if your skin is irritated before you use a chemical peel, the chemicals may penetrate too deeply. A rarer effect is swelling followed by skin darkening. This may happen if a superficial peel is started at a low dose and then increased steadily.
Elena Volf / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
Average cost
The cost
of chemical peels is about $690.
Andrii Sedykh / iStock / Getty Images
DERMAPLANING
Expand
Dermaplaning explained
Dermaplaning is a physical method of exfoliation
,
as opposed to chemical peels, which are chemical exfoliants. Dermaplaning can be used with other cosmetic treatments, such as a collagen or hyaluronic acid mask. This painless technique is performed by an aesthetician  who uses a scalpel to shave the face, removing dead skin and fine hairs and leaving a brighter, clearer, smoother complexion.
Pictured: "StackedSkincare" dermaplaning tool, Sephora
Expand
Risks
Risks are few,
although it is not advised that you do it at home because of a potentially higher risk of infection and nicks.
Erstudiostok / iStock / Getty Images Plus

Average cost
The cost of dermaplaning
averages about $100 per treatment, and repeat treatments are needed every three to four weeks.
Andrii Sedykh / iStock / Getty Images
MICRODERMABRASION
Expand
Microdermabrasion explained
Many physician offices and spas
offer this quick, painless cosmetic treatment that does not require a doctor and has a mild effect on fine lines, wrinkles, and age spots.
Microdermabrasion is a physical method for superficially removing the top layers of the epidermis by lightly sanding the skin with aluminum hydroxide crystals or other similar crystals applied under high pressure. It doesn’t hurt, and some people say it feels like you're being licked by a cat’s sandpapery tongue.
Each treatment takes about 20 to 30 minutes. Typically, you’ll schedule a series of six treatments, one every two weeks, and then periodic follow-up treatments to maintain the effect. Microdermabrasion treatments can be alternated with chemical peels for improved appearance.
dimid_86 / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
Side effects
You may have some redness or swelling
around the eyes, but it usually subsides the same day.
Elena Volf / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
Average cost
A single microdermabrasion
treatment costs $175 on average.
Andrii Sedykh / iStock / Getty Images
MICRONEEDLING
Expand
Microneedling explained
P
erformed in a doctor’s office or spa,
this technique is used to treat fine lines and wrinkles. A doctor repeatedly applies an electric or battery-operated instrument containing multiple small, thin, sharp needles to the skin to puncture it, creating tiny injuries that will stimulate the production of collagen and elastin. (Examples of the instrument doctors use: Collagen P.I.N., DermaFrac, Dermapen, Eclipse MicroPen, Rejuvapen, and SkinPen.)
Therapeutic substances, such as hyaluronic acid, ascorbic acid, or your own platelet-rich plasma can be applied before or after needling so the substance penetrates deeply. The procedure takes 15 to 30 minutes on the face and feels like sandpaper rubbing against the skin. A topical anesthetic can be applied if you feel significant discomfort.
Marcinm111 / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
Side effects
After a microneedling session
, the treated skin may look red or puffy for a couple of days. Risk of infection is minimal, since the instruments are sterilized before treatment. There's a small risk that the therapeutic substance might be absorbed throughout your body.
Your skin may look smoother right away, and its appearance may continue to improve for up to a year. A series of two to three treatments spaced six to eight weeks apart is typically recommended.
Microneedling can be performed along with radiofrequency. Microneedling instruments are also sold for home use (see our lesson “
Home Treatments and Devices
”).
Moussa81 / iStock / Getty Images Plus

The cost
of microneedling is about $300 per session.

BASAL CELL AND SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMAS
Both these types of skin cancer
have been on the rise for many years. This is probably because of increased detection, more sun exposure, and an aging population. More than 3 million men and women are diagnosed with nonmelanoma skin cancer in the United States each year. But only about 2,000 people die from these cancers annually, and most of these deaths are among people whose immune systems are compromised by age or disease.
Click on the "Expand" buttons in the boxes below for more information.
SYMPTOMS TO WATCH
Signs of basal cell carcinoma:
• Small, smooth white or pink bump.
• May become an ulcer or open sore.
• Less commonly, a small, flat, red spot or recurring scar.
Signs of squamous cell carcinoma:
• Raised crusty bump.
• Size ranges from that of a pea to a walnut.
• Less often, a flat or slightly elevated area of skin.
Top: jax10289 / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Bottom: sdigital / iStock / Getty Images Plus
CARCINOMA BASICS
Expand
Basal cell carcinoma
The least dangerous form of skin cancer
—and the least likely to spread to other parts of the body—is basal cell carcinoma. It’s also the most common form, accounting for about 80% of cases.
Basal cell carcinoma originates in basal cells located deep in the epidermis. The most frequent cause of this cancer is cumulative damage from sun exposure. A basal cell carcinoma may first appear as a pearly pimple or bump, a white- or yellow-colored scar, or a scaly red patch.
Some basal cell cancers appear as an ulcer that won’t heal. Basal cell cancer is very slow-growing and will not develop into melanoma, the most deadly form of skin cancer.
Expand
Squamous cell carcinoma
About 20% of skin cancers are squamous cell carcinomas.
This form of cancer arises from flat, scale-like cells in the epidermis. Although squamous cell cancer usually isn’t fatal, it can be life-threatening if it spreads to lymph nodes or internal organs. But even then, the cure rate is around 50%.
More men than women develop squamous cell carcinoma, probably because men tend to get more sun exposure. It usually starts as a small, scaly bump and grows slowly until it resembles an ulcer or wart.
Squamous cell cancers frequently occur on the face, lips, ears, or backs of the hands, and they too result from cumulative sun damage. Early detection is key to preventing the spread of squamous cell cancer. Like basal cell cancer, it will not develop into melanoma.
Expand
Treating basal cell and squamous cell cancers
Options for treating basal and squamous cell cancers are similar.
Which treatment is best for you depends on factors such as the size and location of the cancer, whether it has returned after earlier treatment, and your age and general health.
If surgery is required, the tumor is carefully removed along with the tissue around it, called the margin, to ensure that all cancer cells have been excised. If malignant cells remain, another procedure will be required. The surgical scar can take several months to heal, especially if a tumor was removed from the face.
TREATMENT OPTIONS
Expand
Excision
The cancerous tissue is cut away
along with a small (3- to 10-millimeter) margin of healthy tissue. Then the skin is stitched closed.
If a large area of skin is removed, skin from elsewhere in the body can be used to cover the area (a skin graft).
Expand
Curettage and electrodesiccation
During this process,
the cancerous tissue is scraped away and an electrode is used to kill any remaining microscopic cancerous cells.
Expand
Cryosurgery
Freezing the cancerous tissues with liquid nitrogen
kills them. This treatment usually is reserved for precancers or very small tumors.
Expand
Radiation
The cancerous tissue is destroyed
with a series of high-energy rays aimed from outside the body.
Expand
Mohs micrographic surgery
The tumor is shaved away in thin layers,
one layer at a time, and each layer is checked immediately under a microscope. This allows the surgeon to preserve as much healthy skin as possible while making sure that all of the cancer is removed. This is the most common treatment for skin cancers on delicate or small areas such as the face and fingers.
Expand
Topical drug treatments
Topical treatments
include anticancer drugs such as fluorouracil or imiquimod applied directly to the skin. This approach usually is reserved for precancers or tumors in the top layer of the skin.

HOW TO DO A SKIN SELF-EXAM
To detect skin cancer early,
examine your skin all over your body and watch for changes over time. By checking your skin regularly, you’ll learn what is normal for you. The best time to check your skin is after a shower or bath. Use a full-length mirror and a hand-held mirror in a room with plenty of light. If you find anything unusual, see your doctor.
The National Cancer Institute recommends the following steps to check yourself from head to toe (click on "Expand" in each box).
QUICK TIP
Write down the dates
of your skin self-exams and make notes about the way your skin looks on those dates. You may find it helpful to take photos so you can check for changes over time.
Ulimi / DigitalVision Vectors
FIVE STEPS
Expand
1. FACE, NECK, EARS, SCALP
Look at your face, neck, ears, and scalp. You may want to use a comb or a blow dryer to move your hair so you can see better.
It may be hard to check your scalp by yourself, so consider asking your spouse or a relative or friend to check through your hair.
Outer column: mapodile / Getty Images E+
Above: Triocean / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
2. FRONT AND BACK
2.	Look at the front and back of your body in the mirror. Then, raise your arms and look at your left and right sides.
Gaiamoments
/ Getty Images E+
Expand
3. ARMS
Bend your elbows. Look carefully at your fingernails, palms, forearms (including the undersides), and upper arms.
Anetlanda / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
4. LEGS, GROIN, AND BUTTOCKS
Check the back, front, and sides of your legs. Also check the skin all over your buttocks and genital area.
JosuOzkaritz / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
5. FEET
Sit and closely examine your feet, including your toenails, the soles of your feet, and the spaces between your toes.
Bymuratdeniz / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
PLUS: CHECK FOR CHANGES
Learn where your moles are and their usual look and feel. Check for anything different, such as:
• A new mole (one that looks different from your other moles).
• A new red or darker-colored flaky patch that may be a little raised.
• A change in the size, shape, color, or feel of a mole.
• A sore that doesn’t heal.
• A new flesh-colored firm bump.

OTHER SKIN-CARE PRODUCTS
Thousands of products and substances
are currently being marketed for skin enhancement. Click on each photograph here to learn about three common categories.
MUDS AND OTHER MINERAL PRODUCTS
MUDS AND OTHER MINERAL PRODUCTS
Salts, muds, and clays
are often purported to have natural minerals that restore youthful softness and luster to your skin. While some of these products may leave your skin with a nice clean feel or smell, it is important to recognize that the molecules in them are generally too large to penetrate the skin. In most cases, there are no published scientific data to back up their claims.
Iuliia Zavalishina / iStock / Getty Images Plus
MASKS
MASKS
Hydrating and firming sheet masks
have become very popular in recent years, in addition to applied masks. They contain a variety of compounds, including botanicals, that may or may not be beneficial to the skin but typically leave it appearing better, at least temporarily.
twinsterphoto / iStock / Getty Images Plus
SKIN-LIGHTENING CREAMS
SKIN-LIGHTENING CREAMS
Hydroquinone is a bleaching agent
that is available in many over-the-counter products and by prescription (Eldoquin Forte, Epiquin Micro) to lighten dark spots on the skin. It is generally considered to be safe to use, but you should apply it only to the affected spots and use sunscreen while you’re using it.
Lightening typically occurs after about four weeks of daily use. If you don’t see a change after three months, consult a dermatologist for other options, such as application of tretinoin and glycolic acid to increase skin cell turnover, which may lighten spots.
Hydroquinone can cause dryness, irritation, redness, and itching, and long-term use may result in blue-black spots. Kojic acid, which is derived from fungi and available over the counter, may also help lighten spots and is a natural alternative to chemical bleaching with hydroquinone. However, as with hydroquinone, people with sensitive skin may find kojic acid irritating; it may also make their skin more prone to sunburn.
simarik / iStock / Getty Images Plus

WHAT TO AVOID IN SKIN LOTIONS
It’s always important
to read the ingredient labels of creams and lotions, especially if you have sensitive skin. Although irritation from moisturizers is uncommon, some products contain substances that can cause problems.
The American Academy of Dermatology advises people with sensitive skin to avoid skin-care products with the ingredients noted here (click on the "Expand" button in each box).
MOISTURIZER NO-NOs
Expand
FRAGRANCES AND PRESERVATIVES
Many fragrances and preservatives can irritate skin.
If possible, use products without them.
The least irritating preservatives are parabens, such as methyl paraben and butyl paraben. These preservatives have been used for more than 80 years and appear to be safe and well tolerated. But parabens bind with human estrogen receptors (although very weakly), and research suggesting possible associations with decreased sperm quality and breast cancer has sparked concern about the chemicals.
Because of customer concerns, many manufacturers have removed them from products, although other studies haven’t confirmed the worrisome findings. While further study is warranted, the current evidence doesn’t support a need to restrict exposure.
P
eopleImages
/ Getty Images E
+
Expand
BOTANICAL OR ANTIBACTERIAL INGREDIENTS
“Natural” ingredients aren’t always benign.
If you have allergies or sensitive skin, you may find that plant extracts cause a reaction. Antibacterial lotions can also be irritating for some people.
Chinnapong / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
SOLVENTS
Chemicals that penetrate the skin
include propylene glycol and ethanol.
A better choice is polyethylene glycol, which does not penetrate the skin.

CELLULITIS
Cellulitis is a common skin infection
that can be serious and even life-threatening if it spreads to your bloodstream. Click on the "Expand" button in each box to learn what causes it, why it can have dangerous consequences, and how to treat it.
SIGNS & SYMPTOMS
These are the common signs of cellulitis:
• Skin that's red, hot, and swollen
• Skin that's painful to the touch
• Skin that's collecting or leaking fluid or has a tight, glossy appearance
• Fever and chills
• Skin sores or ulcers surrounded by redness.
Harvard Health Publishing
CAUSES AND TREATMENT
Expand
HOW DO WE GET CELLULITIS?
Cellulitis occurs
when bacteria that normally live on your skin and in your nose and mouth enter the body through a cut or opening in the skin, causing pain, redness, and swelling. If not treated promptly, the infection can reach the bloodstream and circulate to other tissues in your body.
Expand
WHERE IS IT MOST LIKELY TO OCCUR?
The most common site of cellulitis infection
is the legs, but it can develop anywhere on the body. People with diabetes, poor blood circulation, recurring athlete’s foot, or liver disease are more prone to cellulitis. So too are people who’ve recently had surgery, have had radiation treatment of the arms or legs, or have compromised immune function because of a disorder like HIV/AIDS.
Expand
DIAGNOSIS
Your doctor will make a cellulitis diagnosis
after examining you and asking you questions about your health. In some cases, a doctor will order a skin biopsy to confirm the diagnosis.
The two most common bacteria that lead to cellulitis are
Streptococcus
and
Staphylococcus aureus
. In most cases, however, doctors aren’t able to identify the bacteria that are causing the infection.
Expand
TREATMENT
If you've been diagnosed with a mild case of cellulitis,
your doctor will give you a prescription for a topical antibiotic like cephalexin (Keflex) or dicloxacillin to be taken for around five to 10 days.
The antibiotic should show signs of working within 24 to 48 hours. If it doesn’t, the infection may be resistant to that antibiotic, and your doctor will prescribe a different drug; it may also turn out that you have a different skin condition and need a different type of treatment.
While you recuperate, you should keep the area clean and dry, and if the cellulitis is on your arm or leg, elevate the limb above your head to reduce swelling.
Severe cases may require intravenous antibiotics and even surgery to drain fluid and pus.
Expand
KEEP A WATCHFUL EYE
Be on the lookout
for the warning signs of cellulitis. If you’ve had it once, your risk of getting it again goes up to 14% in the first year after your initial infection. Over three years, 45% of people will develop cellulitis a second time.

SKIN RESURFACING, PART 2
Chemical peels, dermaplaning, microdermabrasion, and microneedling
were front and center In our previous lesson. Here, we pick up on skin resurfacing methods by examining laser resurfacing, fractional laser resurfacing, and nonfictional ablative laser resurfacing. Click on the "Expand" button in each box below.
LASER RESURFACING
Expand
Laser resurfacing explained
Improvements in laser technology
have created many options in the treatment of photoaged skin. Lasers remove moderate to deep lines and wrinkles while significantly improving skin tone, texture, and tightness. Their ability to target very specific types of cells in distinct skin layers enables them to treat conditions that were previously impossible to treat, such as port-wine stains, pigmented birthmarks, and spider veins. They can also erase acne pits and many other scars.
Expand
How it works
Lasers work by emitting intense beams of bright “light”
that transfer high levels of energy to a target in or on the skin. There, the energy is converted to heat. Each laser produces a specific single wavelength, or color, of light—from the invisible infrared to visible reds, yellows, and greens. A laser’s wavelength influences how deeply it penetrates the skin and how it will affect the skin.
Different wavelengths work best on different problems. For example, yellow and green wavelengths target the red pigments of port-wine stains or spider veins, while infrared lasers are typically used to remove wrinkles, fine lines, and crow’s feet.
Lasers also vary by the duration of their pulses. The duration of the pulse of light determines how a laser affects targeted tissue. Almost all cosmetic procedures require lasers that emit pulsed light, which confers the precise control needed to alter only the intended skin layers or pigments and leave the surrounding skin unaffected.
Expand
Recovery time
Most laser procedures
are bloodless and can be controlled precisely, reducing the risk for injury or scarring. Recovery times vary depending on the laser used and the depth of treatment.
FRACTIONAL LASER RESURFACING
Expand
Types of fractional laser resurfacing
Fractional laser resurfacing
has generally replaced older methods of laser resurfacing
because it improves the skin’s texture and appearance with a relatively short recovery time.
Instead of removing the entire surface of the skin, this method treats a fraction of the skin’s surface. The laser produces microscopic columns of heat damage, leaving the surrounding skin cells intact. The body’s natural healing process creates new, healthy tissue to replace the columns of affected tissue—improving the skin condition being treated.
There are two types:
ablative fractional resurfacing
and
non-ablative
. (Ablative techniques are so-called because they ablate—that is, remove or destroy—the surface of the skin.) Both offer the advantage of shorter recovery time with lowered risk of scarring, changes in pigmentation, or infection compared with the older technique of nonfractional resurfacing.
Because of these attributes, fractional resurfacing is also safer than other methods for people with darker skin.
Expand
Fractional non-ablative laser resurfacing
This procedure does not remove surface skin
but rather targets deeper skin layers, which then heal by forming new collagen. Experts typically recommend four to six treatments, which are usually done four to six weeks apart. For these treatments, you will need only topical anesthesia to control pain. Some doctors also use a hand-held cool-air device that chills the skin and makes the procedure more comfortable. Your skin will be red for up to a week, and you may have mild swelling for a couple of days.
You may notice some reduction of wrinkles and improvement in skin texture, pigmentation, and tightness almost immediately. Each treatment stimulates the process of new collagen formation, and results are progressive, with the best results appearing in about two to three months. Repeat treatments on a regular basis may be used to maintain the effects. The cost is roughly $1,100 or more for each treatment in a series. Non-ablative lasers include Clear + Brilliant, Fraxel Dual, Fraxel Restore, and Palomar Lux 1540.
Expand
Fractional ablative laser resurfacing
This procedure destroys (ablates) targeted skin on the surface and lower levels,
which is then replaced by new, healthy collagen and epidermal tissue. It can tighten skin or remove spots with a single treatment, which costs somewhere around $2,100, depending on the number and size of the sites being treated. That said, a single, higher-dose treatment requires greater healing time than multiple, lower-dose treatments.
Itching and swelling last up to one week, but mild redness can persist for months. Other possible side effects include scarring, changes in skin color, or a flare-up of the herpes virus (a virus that causes cold sores) in people who carry that infection.
Examples of fractional ablative lasers include Active FX, Deep FX, Fraxel Repair SST, and SmartXide DOX.
Expand
Combination therapy
Both fractional non-ablative laser resurfacing and fractional ablative laser resurfacing
(or a single, dual-mode instrument that incorporates both) also can be used during a treatment. This approach draws on the strengths of each laser, but there still may be substantial side effects, such as scarring or pigment changes.
Expand
Average cost
The cost for ablative laser resurfacing
varies by the type of laser and the treated area, but averages around $675 for a portion of the face and up to $5,800 for the full face.
NONFRACTIONAL ABLATIVE LASER RESURFACING
Expand
Multiple types of nonfractional ablative laser resurfacing
Pulsed or scanned nonfractional ablative laser resurfacing
was once the only laser treatment for cosmetic uses. It's now reserved for advanced photodamage and severe acne scars. It is most effective against damage to the epidermis and superficial layers of the dermis, including lines and wrinkles from sun damage, acne scars, actinic keratoses, freckles, age spots, and dull patches caused by photodamage.
Nonfractional ablative laser resurfacing cannot remove very deep lines and wrinkles, however. For those, you may need soft-tissue fillers.
Recovery time for nonfractional ablative resurfacing can be lengthy. Potential side effects include permanently lightened skin, prolonged redness and inflammation, infection, and scarring. Other, less common complications include acne and darkened skin.
Expand
Carbon dioxide laser resurfacing
Carbon dioxide (CO
2
) laser resurfacing
penetrates more deeply than the technique known as erbium:YAG laser (see below). It also creates more heat damage in the dermis, so it produces the most dramatic results in erasing deep lines and wrinkles.
In addition, carbon dioxide (CO
2
) laser resurfacing can tighten the skin significantly. The intense heat of the CO
2
laser is painful, but you’ll receive a local anesthetic to blunt the pain.
Because this laser sometimes lightens skin, it requires extra caution when treating people with olive-colored or dark skin.
Expand
Erbium:YAG laser resurfacing
This procedure generates less heat and removes a thinner skin layer on one pass.
It is therefore less painful, and recovery time is shorter.
Because the erbium:YAG laser penetrates the skin only about one-fifth as deeply as the CO2 laser, it is usually selected for treating less severe lines and wrinkles. With one or two passes, the erbium:YAG laser moderately improves severe photoaging and eliminates many wrinkles. But your doctor can also make multiple passes to achieve the same dramatic results as with the CO2 laser.
Expand
Combination therapy
Both fractional non-ablative laser resurfacing and fractional ablative laser resurfacing
(or a single, dual-mode instrument that incorporates both) can also be used during a treatment. This approach draws on the strengths of each laser, but there still may be substantial side effects, such as scarring or pigment changes.
Expand
Average cost
The cost for ablative laser resurfacing
varies by the type of laser and the treated area, but averages around $675 for a portion of the face and up to $5,800 for the full face.

HOW TO QUIT SMOKING
Trying to quit smoking?
Research shows that these steps can help you succeed. The goal, remember, is not simply to cut back, but to give it up completely.
1. GET READY: PICK A DATE
1. GET READY: PICK A DATE
The first step if you're determined to quit smoking: Set a date.
Leading up to that date, prepare yourself to kick the habit. The steps ahead will help you stick to it.
© Ruslan Zagidullin | Dreamstime.com
2. REMOVE REMINDERS AND TEMPTATIONS
2. REMOVE REMINDERS AND TEMPTATIONS
Leading up to the "Quit Smoking" date you set
, be sure to remove all cigarettes and ashtrays from your home, car, and place of work.
© Photowitch | Dreamstime.com
3. FIND SUPPORT AND ENCOURAGEMENT
3. FIND SUPPORT AND ENCOURAGEMENT
Enlist the support of family and friends.
Ask them not to smoke around you and to encourage you to succeed. If you have friends trying to quit, encourage them. One study found that smoking behavior spreads through both close and distant social ties; your resolve can help your friends.
© Rawpixelimages | Dreamstime.com
4. REDUCE YOUR STRESS
4. REDUCE YOUR STRESS
Address stress!
Find new and creative ways to manage your stress level: Take regular walks. Work out at a gym. Engage in a new hobby. Join a volunteer group. Know another smoker who's quitting? Ask about his most effective coping skills.
© Iakov Filimonov | Dreamstime.com
5. TALK IT OUT
5. TALK IT OUT
Enlist the help of professionals
who understand nicotine addiction. Find counseling (individual, group, or telephone) with which you're comfortable. Resources include national "quitlines." Dial
800-QUIT-NOW
(800-784-8669) to find one in your state.
© Lightfieldstudiosprod | Dreamstime.com
6. TRY NICOTINE REPLACEMENT THERAPY
6. TRY NICOTINE REPLACEMENT THERAPY
Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT)
can ease cravings, wean you from smoking, and, statistically, double your chances of quitting. Examples: gum,  nicotine patches, lozenges, nasal sprays, and inhalers. Unlike smoking, NRT produces relatively constant blood levels of nicotine without tobacco's toxic chemicals.
© Pixinoo | Dreamstime.com
7. CONSIDER MEDICATIONS
7. CONSIDER MEDICATIONS
If necessary, talk to your doctor about medications
that may help you quit smoking. Two prescription drugs approved as smoking cessations aids: bupropion (Wellbutrin, Zyban) and varenicline (Chantix).
© Simpsonic | Dreamstime.com
8. ALTERNATIVE THERAPIES
8. ALTERNATIVE THERAPIES
Consider alternative therapies
to help you quit. Herbal remedies (including lobelia, St. John's wort, and ginseng), acupuncture, and hypnosis are examples. There's no solid evidence these methods work, but tobacco's health risks are so great that if an alternative therapy is safe and helps you quit, it's worth trying.
© Jochenschneider | Dreamstime.com
9. BE RESOLUTE
9. BE RESOLUTE
Stay determined and resolute.
Be prepared; difficult situations will arise. If you try to quit smoking and fail, don't be discouraged. A first attempt shouldn't be your only attempt; the techniques here are worth trying again.
© Wavebreakmedia Ltd | Dreamstime.com

CONTACT DERMATITIS
As the name implies,
contact dermatitis—a type of rash—occurs after a person’s skin comes in contact with an offending substance. Contact dermatitis can be caused either by an
allergic reaction
or by simple
irritation
from things such as detergents or harsh soaps. All told, more than 3,700 contact allergens have been identified.
Poison ivy and poison oak are classic examples of allergens that can provoke allergic contact dermatitis. Brush against one of these plants and, within a few days, you may develop itchy, red blisters. However, allergic contact dermatitis can also develop unexpectedly, even after you’ve been around a substance for some time with no problem. For instance, nail polish, nickel in jewelry, and certain medications—such as topical antibiotics and anesthetics—can suddenly cause a reaction.
Click on the "Expand" links below to learn more about contact dermatitis triggers (both allergen- and irritant-related) along with treatment.
SIGNS & SYMPTOMS
These are the common signs of contact dermatitis:
• Itchy, red skin
• Irritated patches of skin with clearly demarcated borders
• Tiny bumps or blisters
• Crusted patches on the skin that can develop a wet (weeping) look
CAUSES, TREATMENTS, AND PREVENTION
Expand
ALLERGEN﻿ TRIGGER
Among the thousands of substances
that can trigger contact dermatitis are these common examples:
• Botanical-based cosmetics that contain substances such as tea tree oil, lavender, and peppermint
• Corticosteroids
• Cosmetics
• Fragrances
• Hair dyes
• Lanolin
• Nickel
• Poison ivy (pictured), poison oak, and certain other plants
• Rubber
• Shaving lotion*
• Sunscreen*
• Topical anesthetics
• Topical antibiotics (Neomycin, Bacitracin).
* These can become a problem in the presence of sunlight.
KatieDobies / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
IRRITANT TRIGGERS
Among the
irritants
that can cause contact dermatitis are these common examples:
• Acids and alkalis
• Detergents
• Environmental chemicals (such as insect spray)
• Ethylene oxide
• Oils and greases
• Soaps
• Solvents.
Kwangmoozaa / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
FIRST-LINE TREATMENT
The first step in treatment of contact dermatitis
is to wash the area thoroughly with mild soap to remove any trace of the substance causing the reaction.
Avoiding the trigger is the next step. If you can’t determine the cause of the allergy or irritation, testing by a dermatologist can help reveal the cause.
While general dermatologists perform most types of patch testing, some dermatologists specialize in contact dermatitis.
Once you know the trigger and can avoid it, you might not need further treatment. Keep in mind that the route of exposure may not always be obvious. For example, the plant oils responsible for a poison ivy rash may go first to the fur of your pet or the fabric of your gardening clothes and then transfer to the surface of your skin.
Contact dermatitis usually clears up in about two to three weeks so long as you avoid further contact with the substance that caused it.
FreshSplash / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
FURTHER TREATMENT
If further treatment is needed,
wet dressings and soothing lotions can help ease itching and other symptoms. Other treatments for contact dermatitis include these:
• Corticosteroid creams or ointments.
Available over the counter in varying strengths, these products ease itching and swelling. But in most cases, only more potent, prescription-strength topical corticosteroids will really help.
• Oral corticosteroids.
For severe cases, your doctor may prescribe prednisone, which is usually tapered off gradually, over about 10 to 14 days, to prevent recurrence of the rash.
• Antihistamines.
Over-the-counter non-sedating antihistamines such as fexofenadine (Allegra, generic), loratadine (Claritin, generic), cetirizine (Zyrtec), and levocetirizine (Xyzal) may help control itching.
Iri-s / iStock / Getty Images

HOME SKIN-CARE TREATMENTS AND DEVICES
Cosmetic treatments you can do at home
are becoming increasingly common and can be bought without a prescription directly from manufacturers, at beauty stores such as Sephora and Ulta, and from other retailers.
Examples of products and devices for skin care and hair removal available for personal use include:
Mild chemical peels,
as pictured below (examples of products available: Caudalie Glycolic Peel, Dr. Dennis Gross Alpha-Beta Peel, and many others)
Microdermabrasion kits
(PMD Personal Microderm devices, RejuvadermMD, Silk’n ReVit Diamond Microdermabrasion System).
g-stockstudio / iStock / Getty Images Plus
D.I.Y. SKIN CARE HOME
Chemical peels and microdermabrasion kits
use the same ingredients as professionals do, but in lower concentrations. They can remove dead skin and diminish scars and fine lines, safely and conveniently, for a fraction of the cost of professional treatment. But the results are less dramatic. They work best for minor skin flaws.
LASER OPTIONS
Home laser, LED light, electrocurrent, and ultrasound treatments
are also less powerful than physician- and aesthetician-delivered therapy, but they can be effective if you have the patience to perform treatments on a frequent basis for many weeks or months. They can also be used to maintain the effects of office treatments. Brand names include:
Lux Renew (ultrasound plus LED lights)
Ziip (an electrical current device)
Baby Quasar Clear Rayz
Dr. Dennis Gross’s SpectraLite devices for the eyes and face
LightStim devices for acne and wrinkles
Skin Inc. Optimizer Voyage Tri-Light
Silk’n Face FX (LED light devices)
Tria Age-Defying Laser (laser to treat fine lines).
Be sure to read and follow directions for these products to use them safely.
Laser hair removal devices (No!No!, Remington, Tria) can be used to permanently remove unwanted black or brown body hair. These devices are not recommended for people who are tan, have darker skin, or have a skin condition.
More recent arrivals on the market are intense pulsed light devices (BoSidin, Braun Silk-Expert, Silk’n) to reduce unwanted hair growth.
MICRONEEDLING ROLLER BASICS
Besides the other items noted in this lesson,
there are a variety of home microneedling rollers that can be used to deliver retinols, moisturizers, and other compounds deeply into the skin. Examples of microneedling rollers include Dermaroller, Rodan and Fields, and Skin Medix.
It’s important to sterilize these rollers with an alcohol bath after every use to prevent infections.

DRY SKIN
A lifetime of sun exposure
damages skin, leaving it thinner and less likely to hold in moisture. Also, aging skin produces less of the natural oils that keep skin lubricated. Some medical conditions—such as hypothyroidism, diabetes, and kidney disease—increase the likelihood of developing dry skin. Click on each photograph below to learn more about dry skin.
TREATING DRY SKIN
TREATING DRY SKIN
The first line of defense
against dry skin
is a moisturizer that softens and smooths skin with water and lipids (fats). Some moisturizers attract water to the skin and seal it in, while others prevent skin from losing water by coating it with a thick, impermeable layer.
A
alvarez / Getty Images E+
TYPES OF PRODUCTS
TYPES OF PRODUCTS
Effective products
for battling dry skin include the following:
• Petrolatum (petroleum jelly).
This waxy, greasy substance—commonly known by its brand name, Vaseline—stops water loss without clogging pores. It can be used by itself, but it's also a component of a number of moisturizers and ointments because it’s inexpensive, hypoallergenic, and effective. Because petroleum jelly doesn’t contain water, it’s best used while the skin is still damp after bathing to seal in moisture.
• Oils.
Moisturizing oils, such as mineral oil, have the same effect as petrolatum without being as greasy. Oils should also be used while the skin is still damp.
• Emollient lotions and creams (commonly called moisturizers).
These products contain both water and oils and are therefore less greasy and more cosmetically appealing than either petroleum jelly or oils. Most also contain an emulsifier to keep the product from separating. Some contain humectants, substances that bind water to the skin and help it absorb moisture. Look for moisturizers that contain at least one of the following ingredients: glycerin, urea, pyroglutamic acid, sorbitol, lactic acid, lactate salts, or alpha hydroxy acids.
Towfiqu ahamed / iStock / Getty Images
PREVENTING FURTHER PROBLEMS
PREVENTING FURTHER PROBLEMS
T
he following changes
to your lifestyle or environment can help alleviate dry skin:
• Wear soft fabrics that won’t scratch or irritate the skin.
• Apply moisturizer after washing your hands.
• Add moisture to the air with a humidifier or a pan of water set atop the radiator.
• In the shower or bath, use water that’s lukewarm instead of hot; hot water can further dry the skin by stripping it of natural oils.
• Choose nondrying soaps that contain no abrasives or irritants. Super-fatted soaps or cleansing bars are less drying than regular, liquid, or antibacterial soaps.
• To retain the water your skin absorbs while bathing, apply moisturizer in the shower or immediately after stepping out. Click to the next photo for more on moisturizers.
Axel Bueckert / iStock / Getty Images
MOISTURIZERS
MOISTURIZERS
Below are moisturizer tips
that'll help you choose a product and use it in an effective way.
• Nivea makes an in-shower moisturizer that you put on and wash off while bathing.
• Curel, Jergens, and other companies produce “wet skin” moisturizers that you apply before toweling off.
• If you’re using a conventional moisturizer (and you have many brands to choose from, including products from Aquaphor, Aveeno, CeraVe, Cetaphil, Eucerin, Neutrogena, Olay, and others), pat your skin dry rather than rubbing it after you bathe, and then apply lotion or cream.
• To reach your back, you might try a spray-on moisturizer from Aquaphor, St. Ives, or Vaseline, but be careful to use sprays in a carpeted area, as they can make the floor slippery.
Prostock-Studio / iStock / Getty Images Plus
DRY SKIN
SIGNS & SYMPTOMS
These are the common signs of dry skin:
• Scaly patches of skin, with or without redness
• Itching
• Overall dryness.
PhanuwatNandee / iStock / Getty Images

LIGHT-BASED THERAPY
These treatments use low-level light
(as opposed to the intense light of a laser) to stimulate the skin’s production of collagen. They are best for addressing mild to moderate skin discolorations and imperfections.
KEEP IN MIND
Remember that the procedures described here
have substantial price tags and potential side effects. The decision to alter your appearance is an important one that should be done in consultation with a doctor who understands and respects your wishes. Be sure you understand all the costs involved and the total time commitment (including recovery).
Understand also how long the treatment’s effects are likely to last and any possible side effects you may experience. And be realistic. You cannot achieve perfection—only improvement.
HOW IT WORKS
Expand
Intense pulsed light therapy
Sometimes referred to as a “photo facial,”
intense pulsed light (IPL) therapy (Cutera Xeo, Icon, others) can improve mild to moderate photodamage to skin color and texture with no recovery time and few complications.
In addition to improving the skin’s texture and tone, pulsed light therapy can diminish freckles, red spots, age spots, and dilated blood vessels. It also may be used to treat unwanted dark hair and the red cheeks or nose seen with rosacea.
• How does it work?
The IPL device is not a laser; rather, it emits bursts of broadband light energy that penetrate the epidermis and dermis and promote new collagen growth. IPL treatment does not need to be performed in a doctor’s office and is often offered in salons and spas.
For the procedure, the practitioner will coat your face with a gel and cover your eyes to protect them from the pulse of light. When the device flashes, you’ll feel a quick zap of heat, like the snap of a rubber band. Each flash treats an area about the size of a quarter, and you may have two or three passes in a 30- to 60-minute session. Afterward, you may have some redness or blotchiness for a day or two.
• Treatment details.
Most practitioners recommend four to six sessions, each costing approximately $350, spaced three or four weeks apart. Improvement is gradual, with most people able to notice a difference after three treatments. Maintenance treatments are required a few times a year. Side effects can include sunburn, loss or increase of skin pigmentation, and scarring.
Nikodash / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
Photodynamic therapy
In addition to treating actinic keratoses,
photodynamic therapy is used to reduce fine lines and blotchiness from photoaging. It's also used to rejuvenate the entire face and treat acne.
• Treatment details.
Photodynamic therapy is best done by a doctor. Each treatment takes 45 to 90 minutes, and one to six treatments may be needed, with a cost of about $500 per treatment.
Pictured: A man receives photodynamic therapy as skin cancer treatment.
RapidEye / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
LED photomodulation
Photomodulation,
which is painless, doesn’t damage the surface or deeper layers of skin. Instead, it signals cells that remodel the collagen in the dermis. It's a reasonably effective way to induce subtle improvements in the skin for people with mild to moderate sun damage.
• How does it work?
LED photomodulation uses light produced by light-emitting diodes. During the procedure, your face will be exposed to continuous or pulsed yellow (known as Gentle Waves) or red (ProLight Red/Infrared) LED light for 30 seconds to three minutes.
• Treatment details.
Photomodulation can complement almost any other procedure on your face and works on all skin types. It can also rapidly treat a large area in one brief session, with no aftereffects. Its effects may be subtle, however, and eight to 10 treatments are usually required, with ongoing treatments needed to maintain the results.
In addition to improving sun damage, LED photomodulation can be used to help speed healing following other laser treatments. The treatment can be done by a doctor, nurse, or medical assistant and costs $75 and up.
Joyce Grace / iStock / Getty Images
Expand
If you’re undecided
Are cosmetic treatments worth the time, money, and potential side effects?
Only you can answer that question. But as these treatments improve, they have in some ways become like sunscreen or topical retinoids in that people have come to rely on semi-regular treatments to help prevent the appearance of aging skin and leave them looking younger and healthier.
For some, the payoff is a sense of better competitiveness in the job market. For others, it’s enhanced self-esteem.
In fact, some research submits that aesthetic procedures—especially botulinum toxin—may lead to unconscious changes in your muscle movement and even your mood. According to doctors, people who have cosmetic procedures often report that people respond to them differently afterward, saying things like “You used to look so angry!”
NADOFOTOS / iStock / Getty Images

SKIN TIGHTENING
Skin-tightening procedures
work by heating the deeper skin layers and stimulating collagen growth without damaging the skin’s outer layer. The therapies use devices that emit radiofrequency, pulsed light, or ultrasound energy. Most target skin around the face and neck and require multiple treatments.
draganab / Stock / Getty Images Plus
RADIOFREQUENCY TREATMENT
Expand
Radiofrequency basics
Radiofrequency treatment—sometimes called a nonsurgical facelift
—is used to tighten lax, sagging skin on cheeks, jowls, and brows. While it can be uncomfortable, it requires little to no recovery time, compared with two to three weeks for facelift surgery. But unlike a surgical facelift, radiofrequency doesn’t lift muscle, so it’s not as useful for people with advanced wrinkling and very saggy skin. It is more helpful to people in their late 30s to early 60s who have mild skin looseness and wrinkling.
Elena Volf / iStock / Getty Images Plus
Expand
How does it work?
A radiofrequency device
delivers an electromagnetic current to the skin, while a cooling treatment tip protects the epidermis. The doctor can control the amount of energy delivered by the device. The skin’s natural resistance slows the flow of electrons, which creates an intense, uniform, and sustained heat to the tissues within the skin. The heat penetrates more deeply into the dermis and subdermal layers than lasers do in resurfacing.
Treatment takes an hour or two, depending on the size of the area being treated and the number of passes the doctor makes. You can get back to your daily life immediately. You may have a little redness for an hour, but rarely any swelling, bruising, or pain. Side effects are uncommon and usually temporary.
Pobytov / DigitalVision / Getty Images
Expand
Who produces radiofrequency devices?
The first FDA-approved radiofrequency device
, Thermage, treats fine lines around the eyes, forehead wrinkles, sagging jowls and cheeks, and other areas on the body.
Today, multiple devices with differing characteristics are made by several different companies. Examples include Aluma, Elos, Evoke, Pellevé, and Renuvion.
Courtesy Thermage
Expand
What does it cost?
Treatment can cost as little as $100,
but depending on how much skin is treated, it can run up to $4,500.
Usually only one treatment is needed. You may see immediate improvement, but the full benefits appear gradually over four to six months as collagen production continues. Results may last for up to two years.
Kateryna Kolesnyk / iStock / Getty Images Plus
OTHER TYPES OF RADIOFREQUENCY TREATMENTS
Expand
Internal radiofrequency
Internal radiofrequency
is a more invasive technique for sagging neck skin. Performed with the ThermiTight device, it costs about $3,250 on average.
How does it work? The doctor numbs the area to be treated, makes a nick in the skin, and inserts a cannula (thin tube) into the skin to deliver the radiofrequency energy deeply into the subdermal layer. Discomfort is minimal.
"Before-and-after" photo courtesy Thermi.com
Expand
Fractional high-intensity focused radiofrequency
Fractional high-intensity focused radiofrequency
involves instruments recently been introduced that combine both radiofrequency and microneedling. Examples of instruments for this procedure: Endymed Intensif and Infini.
The microneedling punctures the skin and allows the radiofrequency energy to be delivered within the skin to tighten it up by stimulating collagen and elastin production.
Discomfort is minimal, and the technique works well for tightening skin on the neck and face. Three treatments are typically needed, at a cost of around $1,200 to 1,600 each.
Photo courtesy Endymed.com
ULTRASOUND THERAPY
Expand
Focused or intense ultrasound therapy
Focused or intense ultrasound therapy—called Ultherapy
—works to tighten skin by causing molecules under the skin to vibrate, creating the heat that stimulates collagen formation.
The ultrasound device is similar to what’s used to create ultrasound pictures of a developing baby, although the sound waves penetrate only about 8 millimeters beneath the skin. But this enables the clinician to target the treatment more precisely.
Photo courtesy Ultherapy.com
Expand
Treatment details
Each treatment
takes about 60 to 90 minutes. Most people just have one treatment, but some benefit from additional sessions. The results appear gradually over two to three months, and sometimes up to six months.
Photo courtesy Ultherapy.com
Expand
Cost, side effects, and recovery
A full face and neck
ultrasound therapy treatment costs about $2,600.
Side effects include discomfort during the treatment, redness that usually goes away within a few hours, and swelling that may last a few days. The treated area may also be tender or tingle when touched for several weeks.
CoffeeAndMilk / iStock / Getty Images Plus
